## 1. The Week in One Paragraph

What's true now that wasn't visible Monday: the "bond selloff as orderly normalization" thesis — the dominant framing debate of the entire week — took an active government intervention and lost within one trading session. Treasury doubled its long-bond buybacks Wednesday night specifically to relieve upward pressure on 30-year yields (WSJ, Wed 8/19; FT, Thu 8/20), yields fell 9bp on the announcement, and by Thursday's close the 10-year had already round-tripped back above where it started, with TwentyFour's Eoin Walsh calling the intervention "a sticking plaster" and RSM's Joe Brusuelas warning any relief "will prove only temporary" absent fiscal consolidation (FT, Fri 8/21). That failure lands on top of a structural reframing — MIT's Caballero and Stanford's Lustig's research, synthesized in Friday's Capital Account column, shows Treasurys have shifted from commanding a "safety premium" to demanding an "absorption premium," with roughly 0.75 of the 2.5-point rise in yields since 2015 attributable to the shift (WSJ, Fri 8/21). The same week that this thesis was breaking in the bond market, the private-credit stress that would be the mechanism of a disorderly unwind stopped being hypothetical: a Hollywood equipment supplier defaulted and was seized (FT, Thu 8/20), two distinct entities in Mark Walter's insurance web disclosed roughly $20bn each in undisclosed related-party loans (WSJ, Mon 8/17; FT, Thu 8/20), and Nvidia's own vendor-financing model — now $500bn across six banks plus a standalone $105bn backstop — was explicitly flagged by FT's Lex as untested in a downturn (Fri 8/21). Iran's war, meanwhile, stopped being a crude-price story and became a shipping-and-diesel-cost story worse than Covid disruption per Argus (FT, Thu 8/20), then widened geographically to an assessed Iranian intent to strike NATO/EU soil in Bulgaria and Cyprus — the first time European territory has appeared as a named contingency target in this six-month war.

## 2. Theme Arcs

**Arc 1 — Treasury's safe-haven status is breaking down structurally, and the week's one policy intervention failed within 24 hours. ESCALATING, unresolved.**
Monday opened with the mechanism, not yet the symptom: Fed Chair Warsh cutting FOMC statement word counts 49% year-over-year and going dot-free, justified by a genuinely damning track record (2 of 46 three-year dot-plot projections correct since 2012) but removing a signal markets have priced off for a decade (WSJ, Mon 8/17). By Tuesday the 30-year hit its highest level in nearly 20 years (5.3%, WSJ) — a post-2007 high confirmed independently by FT the same day (5.29%), alongside German bunds at a post-2011 high (3.74%) and Japan's 10-year nearing a "critical" 3% fiscal-credibility line (FT, Tue 8/18). Wednesday's WSJ ran the fight over the number as the story itself: the front page calls it a "rout," the opinion page calls it "normalization," while French OATs (4.105%, highest since 2008), UK gilts (5.333%) and Japan (highest since 1996) confirmed this as a global, not US-specific, move (WSJ, Wed 8/19). Thursday brought the policy response — Treasury doubling buybacks of 10-30yr paper, which knocked the 30-year down 9bp to 5.19% and drew a "middle of the road" $16bn 20-year auction (FT, Thu 8/20) — and Friday brought its failure: the 10-year was back to 4.697% by Thursday's close per WSJ, and FT's Friday edition states yields had "round-tripped most of the prior day's relief" with UK 30-year gilts (5.81%) now trading *higher* than US 30-years (5.24%), evidence this is a G7-wide repricing no single-country buyback program can address.

**Arc 2 — The Iran war's transmission channel shifted from crude price to physical logistics, then widened geographically to NATO/EU territory. ESCALATING, structural not episodic.**
Monday's framing was still containment-focused: a blockade-and-sanctions strategy, Hengli Group's $30bn+ documented Iranian-oil purchases exploiting a narrow sanctions scope, and a 260+-day USS Lincoln deployment straining crew endurance (WSJ, Mon 8/17).
