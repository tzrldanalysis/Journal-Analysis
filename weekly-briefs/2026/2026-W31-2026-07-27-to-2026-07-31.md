## 1. The Week in One Paragraph

The AI-capex trade stopped being a single story on Monday and split into two by Friday: Microsoft added the largest single-day market-cap gain of any US stock in history ($442–450bn, +15.3%) on real, monetizing backlog growth (WSJ, Fri 7/31; FT, Fri 7/31), while Meta's free cash flow went negative for the first time since its 2012 IPO, Alphabet burned cash as a public company for the first time ever, and a leveraged AI-thematic hedge fund (Situational Awareness) was forced into an emergency $16–20bn liquidation to Citadel (WSJ, Fri 7/31; FT, Fri 7/31). That bifurcation was visible in embryonic form Tuesday (CXMT's IPO pop read skeptically by FT's Lex, FT, Tue 7/28) and hardened daily through hyperscaler CDS spreads hitting record highs (FT, Wed 7/29) and Oracle's downgrade to one notch above junk (FT, Wed 7/29; WSJ, Fri 7/31). Running in parallel, not incidental to it, Fed Chair Warsh's deliberate-silence strategy was tested by Wednesday's meeting and failed: three regional presidents dissented for a hike (the first same-direction three-way dissent since 2016), Warsh offered "no magic wand," and the bond market did the tightening he wouldn't — the 30-year hit its highest yield since 2007 by Friday, which a fixed-income strategist described as the market having "punch[ed] him in the face" (WSJ, Fri 7/31). What is true now that wasn't visible Tuesday: this is no longer two separate stories about tech valuations and Fed communication — it is one story about who bears the rising cost of capital when the AI-financed economy's exposed edges (leveraged funds, junk-adjacent bond issuers, a silent central bank) are tested simultaneously by a live war that will not stay contained to one theater.

## 2. Theme Arcs

**Arc 1 — AI capex is bifurcating on cash-flow discipline, and by Friday the market had already priced the split (escalating, resolving into a bifurcation rather than a single crash).**
Tuesday opened with CXMT's 466% Shanghai IPO pop read skeptically by FT's own Lex column — a liquidity/underpricing artifact, not a re-rating (FT, Tue 7/28) — while WSJ covered the same debut as a national-security reckoning for Apple and Nvidia (WSJ, Tue 7/28). By Wednesday this had moved from equity narrative to credit fact: hyperscaler CDS spreads hit record highs across Oracle, Alphabet, Meta, Amazon and Nvidia, Oracle was downgraded to BBB- after a $70bn capex commitment, and Société Générale's Manish Kabra stated the shift explicitly — "For hyperscalers, watch CDS, not EPS" (FT, Wed 7/29). WSJ's own Wednesday edition independently confirmed the credit lens: Meta's El Paso bond priced ~275bp over Treasuries, wider than a comparable deal a year earlier despite unchanged ratings (WSJ, Wed 7/29). Thursday escalated further — Meta cut the low end of 2026 capex guidance to $115bn from $125bn as free cash flow collapsed toward $784mn, even as Microsoft's Azure accelerated to 43% growth (WSJ, Thu 7/30) — and FT reported the BoE's Prudential Regulation Authority formally probing UK prime-broker concentration in Asian AI equities, plus the largest hedge-fund collateral calls since 2016 (FT, Thu 7/30). By Friday the split was total and named: Microsoft's historic surge against Meta's, Alphabet's and Amazon's cash-flow deterioration, plus the first concrete forced-unwind event (Situational Awareness) in the trade (WSJ, Fri 7/31; FT, Fri 7/31). As of Friday's edition this is not resolved — it is a live bifurcation with one clear near-term test (whether the Situational Awareness liquidation is idiosyncratic or the first domino) still open.

**Arc 2 — Fed credibility under Warsh's silence strategy broke on contact with a real shock, and the bond market is now pricing the Fed instead of the other way around (resolved, unfavorably, by Friday).**
Tuesday's frame was that Warsh's deliberate opacity was untested and Governor Waller had already broken with it publicly (WSJ, Tue 7/28). Wednesday's Fed decision landed with markets pricing a one-in-three chance of a *hike*, not a cut, and NY Fed President Williams pointedly said credibility comes from following data, not managing perception (WSJ, Wed 7/29) — the same day BIS and Singapore's MAS separately warned that AI-driven data-centre spending is distorting the very productivity signals Warsh's rate-cut case depends on (FT, Wed 7/29). Thursday's actual vote — a hold with three regional presidents (Hammack, Kashkari, Logan) dissenting for a hike, the first three-way same-direction dissent since 2016 — produced a textbook steepener: the 2-year fell while the 30-year jumped 13.6bp to 5.228%, its biggest one-day move in over a year and highest level since 2007 (WSJ, Thu 7/30). By Friday this had become explicit market revolt: 30-year yields at 5.24%, ING's Francesco Pesole calling it a "loss-of-confidence trade," and a strategist quoted describing the bond market's response as having "punch[ed] him in the face" (FT, Fri 7/31; WSJ, Fri 7/31). This arc is resolved for the week — the silence strategy has visibly failed on its own terms — but the consequence (a persistently higher long end feeding into mortgage rates and AI-capex funding costs simultaneously) is now a live input into Arc 1, not a separate story.

**Arc 3 — The Iran war escalated from a de-escalation bet to a multi-theater energy-security war, and China's demand suppression is the only thing keeping it from becoming a 2022-style price shock (escalating, unresolved).**
Tuesday's market was pricing de-escalation — oil fell over the strike pause even as Saudi facilities took drone fire the same day, a gap neither paper's markets desk reconciled (WSJ, Tue 7/28; FT, Tue 7/28). Wednesday inverted this: Iran struck US forces in Jordan unprompted — a break from its prior retaliation-only pattern — and rejected Oman's proposed Hormuz split outright, while WSJ's own reporting showed Iranian crude still reaching China via Houthi-granted "safe passage" even as the blockade rhetoric held (WSJ, Wed 7/29). Thursday brought the US retaliatory strikes Trump had threatened ("a beating"), Brent +6.6–7.9%, and the more structural fact of the week: the US Strategic Petroleum Reserve fell to 307.7mn barrels, a 40-plus-year low, against an estimated 180–200mn barrel operational floor, with China's 40% cut in crude imports functioning as the actual global shock absorber — one Beijing can sustain for roughly six more months on stockpiles (WSJ, Thu 7/30; FT, Thu 7/30). Friday showed the war widening geographically rather than cooling: a drone strike hit Damietta, Egypt — the first targeting of Egypt in the war, against a corridor built specifically to bypass Hormuz — alongside Kuwait infrastructure strikes and contained but real overnight US strikes on Iran that got a single line of coverage despite driving Shell's near-record trading profit the same day (WSJ, Fri 7/31; FT, Fri 7/31). This arc is not resolved; it is structurally worse than Tuesday's framing assumed, and China's stockpile buffer — not diplomacy — is the thing standing between the current price range and a much sharper shock.

**Arc 4 — EU sanctions and competition orthodoxy are visibly bending to industrial-policy and commercial pressure, a genuine regime shift rather than a one-off exception (confirmed, still developing).**
[WEAK SIGNAL upgraded.] Tuesday's FT flagged Greece's Dynagas carve-out as the first acknowledged weakening of the EU's Russia sanctions program in 21 rounds, paired with the Aughinish Alumina non-decision on a Rusal-linked alumina plant (FT, Tue 7/28) — a single-story item at that point. What started as a single-day item about one Greek carve-out is, by Friday, a named cross-week theme with a direct market read-through (see Section 5, MP reversal).

## 10. Steel-Man of the Week

**Consensus view:** The AI capex boom cracked this week — record hyperscaler CDS spreads, Oracle's downgrade, a chip-stock rout, and a forced hedge-fund liquidation together describe the start of a systemic AI-financing unwind.

**Steel-man:** Read across the full week rather than any single day, the more defensible claim is that markets successfully and rapidly re-priced by balance-sheet quality — which is a healthy function, not a crisis. Wednesday's own FT steel-man supplied the first counter-evidence: Man Group's record AUM, Barclays' underlying profit growth, and CMA CGM's strong volumes all landed the same day as the CDS story, and every CDS level cited that week remained investment-grade — Oracle's downgrade landed at BBB-, a notch above junk, not into it (FT, Wed 7/29). Thursday's FT steel-man added that Singapore's AI-driven export data was still growing (over 70% of this year's export growth), meaning real, not just speculative, demand persisted even as credit spreads widened (FT, Thu 7/30). Friday supplied the actual resolution the earlier steel-mans could only argue for hypothetically: Microsoft did not merely avoid the "crash" — it delivered the single largest one-day market-cap gain by any US company in history, on disclosed $130bn of new leases and a cloud backlog that grew $51bn in a quarter (WSJ & FT, Fri 7/31), while the PHLX Semiconductor index rallied 8.19% the same session (WSJ, Fri 7/31). A genuine systemic credit event does not typically coincide with a historic single-stock rally and a broad chip-sector rebound in the same week. The more accurate weekly narrative, visible only by reading Wednesday's hedge and Friday's resolution together, is bifurcation-by-balance-sheet, not contagion — though this reading has a real limit: Situational Awareness's forced liquidation (Fri) is the first concrete evidence that leveraged positioning inside the trade can still produce disorderly, not just orderly, unwinds, and one week of repricing does not prove the leveraged edges of the trade are safe going forward.
