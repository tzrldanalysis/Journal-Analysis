## 1. The Week in One Paragraph

What was true by Friday that wasn't true Monday morning: the market stopped debating whether the Fed's next move is a cut and started pricing a hike outright, and it did so as a global, synchronized move rather than a US-specific one — UK gilts and German Bunds hit multi-decade highs alongside Treasurys, the ECB hiked into it, and FT's own Friday column reframed the entire selloff as structural (a permanent shift in who buys sovereign debt) rather than a cyclical CPI bet. That repricing didn't happen in isolation — it rode on top of an Iran/Gulf war that widened rather than contained itself, adding a second maritime chokepoint (Bab al-Mandeb) to the first (Hormuz) by Friday, with Brent closing the week at $107.63 against roughly $91-98 at the week's start. The two arcs reinforced each other all week: energy-driven inflation fed the hawkish Fed repricing, and the repricing itself is now happening faster than either Treasury's buybacks or the ECB's messaging can contain.

## 2. Theme Arcs

**Arc 1 — The "next Fed move is a cut" prior didn't just weaken this week, it broke, and did so as a globally synchronized event.** It started narrowly: Wednesday's WSJ flagged the 10-year at 4.805%, its highest close since October 2023, purely as a US oil-driven inflation story (WSJ, Wed 9/9), while Wednesday's FT was still framing elevated yields as a UK-specific gilt story (5.82%, highest since 1998) tied to the government's regional-growth spending (FT, Wed 9/9). By Thursday the US story sharpened into a Fed-specific bet: hike odds jumped to 60% from 35% pre-Jackson Hole, Bessent's $6bn buyback operation failed to move the tape, and Oracle got flagged as the AI-credit "weakest link" the same day (WSJ, Thu 9/10). Friday fused both threads into one: US hike odds hit 71%, the 10-year closed at 4.943% and the 30-year approached 5% (WSJ, Fri 9/11), while FT's edition showed the US 50-year at 5.35% (highest since 2007), UK 10-years at 5.38% (highest since 2007) and German 10-years at 3.5% (post-2011 high) — with the ECB itself hiking to 2.5% the same day (FT, Fri 9/11). FT's Benn Steil column supplied the structural reframe: the share of Treasurys held by price-insensitive buyers has fallen from 76% to 24% over 19 years, meaning the selloff isn't a CPI-bet that reverses on one print but a mechanical consequence of who's left holding the debt (FT, Fri 9/11). **Status: escalating, unresolved** — next week's FOMC decision now has to answer a question the market has already answered for it.

**Arc 2 — Iran's Gulf war is repricing oil as a structural, widening risk, not a contained shock.** Tuesday's WSJ opened with an internal contradiction: a naval blockade documented as trapping 85% of Iranian Gulf oil loadings, sitting three pages from the paper's own investigation showing $9bn moved through US correspondent banks in 2024 alone (WSJ, Tue 9/8). Wednesday brought a genuine cross-publication split (detailed in Section 8) — WSJ reported Brent near $98 and Hormuz transits collapsed to single digits a day, both banks and shippers revising risk upward (WSJ, Wed 9/9), while FT's own numbers for the same day showed Brent flat at $69.71 even as it covered the identical Hormuz mine standoff (FT, Wed 9/9). Thursday resolved the ambiguity emphatically: Brent briefly topped $100 for the first time since July, Iran fired maneuverable-warhead missiles at US warships for the third time in a week, and European gas hit its highest level since early 2023 on a 15-year-low storage position (WSJ, Thu 9/10). Friday added the structural widening: Iran-backed Houthis seized the Yemeni port of Mocha, opening a path to a second chokepoint at Bab al-Mandeb, pushing Brent to $107.63 (+6.3%) (WSJ, Fri 9/11), while FT reported Iran's central bank quietly encouraging crypto (Tether/bitcoin) settlement to bypass sanctions — financial adaptation running in parallel with the military one (FT, Fri 9/11). **Status: escalating, unresolved.**

**Arc 3 — AI capex is being re-categorized from an equity growth story to a credit/leverage story, and by Friday the financing risk is visibly diffusing into balance sheets that were never built to carry it.** Wednesday is where both papers independently made the same pivot: WSJ's edition documented Salesforce, ServiceNow, Snowflake and Workday all beating and citing AI as a tailwind, undercutting the software-disintermediation thesis — except Shopify, the most AI-native operator in the group, fell 7% the same day in a "broader software selloff" the paper couldn't explain (WSJ, Wed 9/9). FT's Wednesday edition went further, reporting Anthropic and OpenAI both lobbying for investment-grade ratings specifically to lower debt costs ahead of IPOs, with "doubt cast on high debt levels" in the same headline, plus Anthropic's own trustees wrestling with governance tension (FT, Wed 9/9). Thursday sharpened this into a single named proxy: Oracle's CDS near a multiyear high, one notch above junk, with S&P explicitly tying Oracle's credit quality to whether OpenAI and Anthropic can keep raising outside financing (WSJ, Thu 9/10). Friday delivered a genuine, unresolved contradiction within the arc: Oracle's own Q1 print was a blowout (cloud infrastructure revenue +121%, RPO $664bn, shares +7%) — the bull case for capex sustainability — landing the same day the Pentagon moved to lend Fluidstack $5bn, Trump declared a national emergency over foreign electrical equipment in data centers, and South Korea's state vehicle neared a $100bn+ AI-power financing deal (WSJ, Fri 9/11). ## 10. Steel-Man of the Week

**Consensus view:** The Iran/Gulf war's widening and the hawkish, now-globally-synchronized Fed/ECB repricing together lock in a durable, structurally higher-rate, risk-off regime through year-end.

**Steel-man:** This narrative is harder to sustain once the week's own contradictions are placed side by side. Fed governor Waller explicitly said as late as Thursday and again Friday that he'd back holding rates steady if core CPI matched the roughly 0.2% month-over-month forecast, and Fed staff models reportedly still expect disinflation to resume as tariff effects "rinse through" — meaning the market's 71% hike-odds pricing is a bet on a single data print, not a settled Fed reaction function (WSJ, Thu 9/10 and Fri 9/11). Lagarde's own ECB explicitly "did not discuss any future rate path" after Friday's hike, directly undercutting the swap market's confident pricing of two more hikes by March 2027 (FT, Fri 9/11). And the week's own equity evidence cuts against a uniform risk-off read: the S&P energy sector was up more than 40% year-to-date with Marathon Petroleum at a record high the same session the Dow fell 628 points (WSJ, Wed 9/9); Signet Jewelers (+24%), Fortum (+16%) and Jersey Mike's (+7%) all rose the same Wednesday the bond selloff intensified (WSJ, Thu 9/10); and Friday's own Oracle beat, Adobe's 1-billion-user milestone, and Positron's $875mn AI-inference raise at a $5bn valuation all argue the capex cycle retains genuine private demand rather than being a purely credit-driven bubble about to reprice broadly (WSJ, Fri 9/11). None of this individually refutes the risk-off narrative — energy strength is itself partly a symptom of the same oil shock driving the inflation fear — but together, the dovish Fed holdout, the ECB's explicit refusal to commit to a path, and the market's own idiosyncratic winners suggest the "durable regime" framing is doing more work than the week's actual, contested evidence supports.
