Before the war, roughly 100 ships a day transited the strait fee-free; a formalized Iranian veto over inbound traffic would functionally create a toll gate on Gulf-bound shipping — a structural, not episodic, leverage point Tehran would retain even after a ceasefire. Market implication: energy-security premium should be sticky even if a truce holds, because the mechanism of control, not just the war, is what's being negotiated. Geopolitical signal: Iran is negotiating for a permanent asset (chokepoint control), not just a ceasefire, which tells you its post-war strategy assumes continued confrontation with the U.S. and Gulf states.

Meanwhile, the sanctions-enforcement mechanics look considerably softer than the rhetoric. Iranian crude reaching China has fallen from a pre-blockade peak of 1.7 million barrels/day (March) to 523,000 bpd in July (Kpler) — real degradation — but Chinese-flagged tankers are still getting explicit "safe passage" from the same Houthi forces attacking Saudi-linked shipping at Bab al-Mandeb, and floating reserves off Malaysia continue to supply Chinese refiners. Market implication: the "blockade" caps volume but doesn't eliminate flow, meaning the ceiling on Iran's foreign-currency earnings from oil is softer than headline chokepoint disruption implies. Geopolitical signal: China is functionally underwriting Iran's sanctions evasion at scale while still buying Saudi crude through the same corridor — a dual-track posture that gives Beijing leverage over both sides of the conflict simultaneously.
