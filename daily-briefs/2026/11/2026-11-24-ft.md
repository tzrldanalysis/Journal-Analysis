## 1. Today's Themes

**Nvidia's board subordinated governance to political pressure, and the market rewarded it instantly.**
The board voted unanimously to reinstate Jensen Huang eleven days after forcing him out over an affair with Alice Huang, a Megaspeed executive US intelligence suspects of acting as a Chinese asset. The board cited "input from senior levels" of the US government and said reinstatement "poses no national security risk" — a claim that contradicts standing US intelligence warnings. The FT's Big Read on Nvidia's governance finds no risk committee, no succession plan, and not one board member with government, defence or intelligence experience. The reaction was a $1tn one-day market-cap gain and a 32–35% share surge that dragged the Nasdaq 100 up as much as 7% and the S&P 500 up 4.74%. FT View, Lex and Robert Armstrong's column all converge on the same read from different angles: Lex calls it explicitly "a win for money over morals"; FT View calls it "a dangerous precedent"; Armstrong argues Huang was reinstated not on merit but because the company had built no bench to replace him.
*[WEAK SIGNAL]* The same pattern — a credentialed, "Western-friendly" intermediary used to launder compliance risk — recurs quietly across the Alice Huang/Megaspeed chip-smuggling network (Inspur→Aivres, Prince Group's "trusted representative" structure) and is structurally identical to the governance workaround at board level. This is a repeatable playbook for moving risk through a trusted face, not a one-off Nvidia problem.

**The AI-trade rally is being propped up by state intervention and sentiment, not by confirmed demand.**
China's "national team" bought a record $13.8bn of shares in a single session; Taiwan banned short selling below the prior close; South Korea activated a stabilisation fund. This followed technical bear markets (>20% declines) in China, Korea and Taiwan, with the Kospi down more than $1.8tn since June and the Taiex shedding over $500bn in a week. Separately, copper — the physical proxy for data-centre build-out — collapsed from above $14,670 to under $12,000 a tonne on doubts about the durability of AI-linked electricity demand, even as traders now reverse last year's tariff-driven US stockpiling (94% of global LME/Comex copper sits in the US, up from 20% two years ago) and ship the metal back to China. CoreWeave, Oracle, Micron, Alphabet and Meta all suffered double-digit-to-70% drawdowns from 12-month peaks before yesterday's bounce.

**Local political backlash is now a binding constraint on US AI infrastructure, not a tail risk.**
Ohio voters passed 21 ballot measures to slow data-centre development, directly threatening $17bn of Amazon's planned spending and 1.8GW of capacity; a further 19 measures passed elsewhere in the US this month. Across the country, roughly 40 measures now target 10GW of proposed capacity, and three sites in Maine and Ohio have already been abandoned by developers. This connects directly to the demand-durability doubts driving the copper collapse and the Asian tech-stock rout — the capex story that underwrites both is now facing a grassroots veto in the jurisdiction that matters most.

**Sovereign stress is being managed through institutional engineering rather than fiscal adjustment, in both France and the Gulf.** *[FT ANGLE]*
Macron floated a constitutional "golden rule" on the deficit with no underlying 2027 budget yet passed; French 10-year yields still fell 9bp to 4.79% purely on the proposal, outperforming Eurozone peers, even though the same benchmark touched 5% only last month and a constitutional amendment needs a three-fifths majority Macron does not command. In parallel, Gulf states are using swap lines (Abu Dhabi's $5bn support for Bahrain), rerouted shipping and diversified infrastructure to paper over a "permanent scar" from the US-Iran Hormuz standoff — Qatari government revenue fell 42% and Kuwait's fell 25% in the period, with Allianz Trade pricing the regional reconstruction bill at $200bn.

**COP31's collapse exposes the limits of consensus-based multilateral diplomacy at the exact moment climate risk is compounding.** *[FT ANGLE]*
The Antalya summit failed to reference "fossil fuels" in its final text after Saudi Arabia and Russia blocked the language, despite a UN report confirming the world is on track to permanently breach the 1.5°C threshold. Pilita Clark's column frames this as a structural crisis for the "only global process" on climate, not a one-off failure — a framing WSJ's more US-policy-centred lens would likely underweight relative to its market implications for carbon-capture names and EU climate-finance flows.

## 3. Geopolitics & Policy

Market implication: expect renewed upward pressure on battery-grade cobalt pricing and tighter EV-OEM cost planning; geopolitical signal: African minerals states are converging on export leverage as a deliberate policy tool, not an ad hoc response. Separately, the copper arbitrage reversal is itself a tariff-mechanics story — traders are now betting Trump's threatened copper tariffs are "dead" following the Democrats' midterm victory, and the resulting flow of US stock back to China (reversing a build to 94% of global LME/Comex inventory) is both a market signal and a read on US trade-policy credibility going into 2027. On export controls proper, the Nvidia chip-smuggling network traced by the FT (Megaspeed→Inspur→Aivres, with Alice Huang as the "trusted" front) shows exactly how OEM loopholes in the entity-list regime are exploited at scale — Nvidia says it now blocks both entities, but the structure demonstrates controls are being arbitraged in real time, not closed.

**Institutional and structural dynamics.** [FT ANGLE] COP31's failure to reference "fossil fuels" in its final text, blocked by a Saudi Arabia-Russia bloc, is being read in London and Brussels as evidence the consensus-based COP model itself may be structurally broken — Al Gore called it "outrageous that a handful of petrostates have veto power," and Ireland's climate minister said the process was "hijacked by fossil fuel producers." The second-order effect is capital-flow relevant: roughly 25 countries have already begun publishing unilateral transition roadmaps outside the COP track, suggesting climate policy and associated capital allocation are moving toward a "coalition of the willing" model rather than a single global framework — a dynamic that favours EU/UK climate-finance vehicles with credible domestic mandates over globally-coordinated ones. Separately, the Japan-China Apec snub (Takaichi isolated while Xi, Trump and Putin held a trilateral that attacked the ICC) is a structural signal that Washington under Trump is willing to let a core Pacific alliance relationship visibly fray for short-term optics with Beijing — a genuine realignment risk for Tokyo that most US-centric coverage would file as a minor diplomatic story rather than the alliance-credibility event the FT treats it as.
