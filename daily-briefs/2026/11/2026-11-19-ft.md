## 1. Today's Themes

**AI credit is bifurcating, and the bifurcation is now visible in spreads, not just equity multiples.**
JPMorgan is offering steep concessions — an 8 per cent coupon at roughly 80 cents on the dollar, a 12–13 per cent all-in yield — to revive a stalled $2bn high-yield sale backing Qualtrics' $6.75bn acquisition of Press Ganey Forsta, after the bridge financing sat unsold since March on "AI disruption" fears. On the same day, Proofpoint priced $2bn of debt with $7bn of orders and a yield that fell from over 9 per cent to about 8.75 per cent. Cross-reference: this sits alongside Goldman, Barclays, Oppenheimer and Morgan Stanley all cutting 2027 earnings-growth forecasts roughly in half (global profit growth to 16 per cent from 33.5 per cent; US to 11–15 per cent from 30–36 per cent) on cooling hyperscaler capex, and the ECB's Financial Stability Review explicitly drawing "parallels with the dotcom and subprime bubbles" in AI-related debt. The market is no longer treating "AI exposure" as a single factor — it is pricing issuer-by-issuer credit quality within it.

**Governance has collapsed at the exact points where AI capital is most concentrated.**
Nvidia has no permanent CEO, only just appointed its first-ever board chair, and is run by directors who have sat for roughly three decades, even as a nepotism scandal (Jensen Huang's daughter, son-in-law and son all holding outsized informal authority over the Megaspeed response) sits alongside an active DoJ/congressional probe and a $1.5tn market-cap loss. Separately, Leopold Aschenbrenner's Situational Awareness fund — rescued by Citadel this summer after a wipeout, with JPMorgan cutting its lending in response — is already discussing re-levering and raising outside capital, barely months after Aschenbrenner's "mea culpa" pledge never to borrow again. Cross-reference: both stories connect to Anthropic's IPO, where Situational Awareness holds a ~$5bn stake (Aschenbrenner's wife is Dario Amodei's chief of staff) and SoftBank's Masayoshi Son publicly regrets not buying in. The pattern is the same: capital and authority concentrated in small, personally-entangled circles with thin external checks.

**Great-power theatre is running ahead of — and in Europe's reading, dangerously detached from — battlefield and economic reality.** [FT ANGLE]
The Trump-Xi-Putin trilateral at the Shenzhen Apec summit, with talk of a "new Yalta and Potsdam" covering European security architecture, comes as Russia's forces control barely a fifth of Ukrainian territory and — cross-reference — Russia has been forced into record seaborne petrol imports (390,000 tonnes in October) after Ukrainian drone strikes crippled refining capacity, reversing its historic role as a fuel exporter. The FT's own leader explicitly frames Putin's inclusion as "a reward for his aggression" with no underlying change in his negotiating position. Europe's countermove — a 35-nation "coalition of the willing" convening in Berlin the same day, plus a fresh €45bn EU loan to Ukraine — is covered as a deliberate structural counterweight, a framing a US-centric paper would likely underplay.

**AI's own tools are simultaneously destabilising critical infrastructure and disrupting the professional-services firms meant to police risk.**
A wave of outages (Microsoft 365 down for ~24 hours across 70+ countries, an FAA flight halt, an NSA internal-network degradation, Chase locking customers out) is attributed not to attack but to AI-accelerated vulnerability discovery (Calif's use of OpenAI's GPT-6 Cyber model found Java flaws used by 90 per cent of Fortune 500 companies) outpacing organisations' ability to patch safely. Separately, KPMG's first AI-based statutory audit "asked better questions" than its own accountants, while the UK's FRC warns AI could pose a "distinct threat" to auditor independence. Same underlying force, two very different stress points — security and assurance — both breaking at once.

**France's fiscal-political risk is re-approaching euro-crisis-era stress levels with no obvious circuit breaker.** [FT ANGLE]
The ECB's Financial Stability Review flags France explicitly: the OAT-Bund spread has risen as high as 1.75 percentage points, the highest since 2011, with French 10-year yields briefly topping 5 per cent for the first time in two decades, driven by "political instability" ahead of April's presidential election. The ECB stresses it sees no present justification for deploying its crisis tool, but notes traders are "wondering" whether it eventually will. Cross-reference: Janan Ganesh's opinion column makes the structural case explicit — the French centre "will never revive until the alternative to it is at last tested in office," implying this is a multi-year, not a near-term, risk.

**[WEAK SIGNAL]** A shadow AI-chip supply chain through Singapore and its South-East Asian neighbours is surfacing across several unconnected stories rather than one big one: Nvidia quietly stopped disclosing Singapore-specific revenue after it hit 72 per cent of global revenue growth and roughly half of worldwide sales; Megaspeed (Singapore-based, Alibaba-linked) is under criminal investigation for alleged chip smuggling to China; and the US has just imposed new licensing requirements on data centres in Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand and Indonesia. No single article calls this a crisis, but the pattern — opacity, geography, and enforcement tightening appearing together — rhymes with a bigger story still forming.

## 3. Geopolitics & Policy

**Trade, tariffs and sanctions mechanics.** The EU Commission is preparing proposals, due December 9, to dramatically toughen its foreign-subsidies regulation: companies found in breach of foreign-subsidy rules in public tenders in one EU member state could be barred from tenders bloc-wide, and bidders on contracts above €250mn would have to disclose financial contributions from non-EU governments. The mechanism is precise and enforcement-tested — the Commission already replaced Chinese subcontractor CRRC on a Lisbon metro tender this year. Market implication: this raises the compliance and disclosure burden on any non-EU (overwhelmingly Chinese) bidder for large European infrastructure work, with second-order effects on construction, transport and energy-equipment supply chains operating in the bloc. Geopolitical signal: this is Brussels building unilateral economic-defence tooling independent of Washington — a structural China de-risking move that long predates and sits outside the US tariff conversation. [FT ANGLE] A WSJ-style read would likely frame EU-China friction through the lens of US tariff policy; the FT treats it as the EU constructing its own instrument of economic statecraft, explicitly "to reduce its dependence on China and other suppliers from outside the bloc."

**Institutional and structural dynamics.** The Trump-Xi-Putin Shenzhen trilateral is the day's dominant structural story. Putin reopened the idea that any Ukraine settlement must revisit European security architecture on Russian terms — his negotiator Vladimir Medinsky explicitly invoked a "new, modern Yalta and Potsdam" in talks last week. The FT View calls this "something that looks rather like his wish" and states plainly that Putin "is in no credible position to dictate terms," given Russia controls barely a fifth of Ukrainian territory after nearly five years of war. [FT ANGLE] This is the clearest instance in the edition of London-vs-Washington framing: where a US outlet might cover the summit as a Trump diplomatic achievement (nuclear non-proliferation, Taiwan, Iran all on the agenda), the FT leader treats Putin's mere inclusion as a reputational and structural reward for aggression, and pairs it explicitly with Europe's countermove — the 35-nation "coalition of the willing" meeting in Berlin the same day and a fresh €45bn EU loan to Kyiv.

## 5. Steel-Man

**Consensus view:** The AI capital-expenditure supercycle is a fundamentally sound, if decelerating, driver of global equity returns and corporate earnings into 2027.

**Steel-man:** The ECB's own Financial Stability Review draws direct "parallels with the dotcom and subprime bubbles," pointing to more than $4tn in off-balance-sheet AI commitments and circular financing where chipmakers fund the very data centres that buy their chips — arrangements the ECB says "inflate the expected revenues" of both sides. Simultaneously, credit markets are already differentiating sharply: Qualtrics needed an 8 per cent coupon and a 20-point price discount to move AI-adjacent software debt, while the entire Wall Street analyst community has cut 2027 earnings-growth forecasts roughly in half. Layer on governance failure exactly at the points of greatest capital concentration — Nvidia's leaderless board, the Aschenbrenner fund's rapid re-leveraging after a near-collapse, SoftBank's belated scramble to buy exposure it missed — and the picture looks less like a durable technological transformation steadily digesting a growth slowdown, and more like a boom whose financial plumbing (circular deals, concentrated personal relationships, opaque disclosure) is the actual load-bearing structure, with earnings fundamentals a secondary justification layered on top.

## 9. Europe Watch

**France.** The ECB's Financial Stability Review puts a hard number on French political risk: the OAT-Bund spread has reached as high as 1.75 percentage points — the highest level since the depths of the 2011 euro crisis — and French 10-year yields briefly broke above 5 per cent last month for the first time in more than 20 years, driven explicitly by "political instability in France ahead of presidential elections" in April. The ECB states it currently sees "no justification" for deploying its emergency bond-buying tool (created in 2022), but notes that "traders and analysts have started to wonder" whether it will eventually be forced to intervene to prevent "a second Eurozone debt crisis." Janan Ganesh's opinion column supplies the structural read: France's political centre, in his argument, cannot durably recover until Marine Le Pen's National Rally is actually tested in office and seen to fail, the way populism has already been tested (and is fading) in Britain and the US — implying this is a risk that persists through, not just up to, April's election, with no faster circuit breaker visible.

**Broader European.** [FT ANGLE] The EU Commission's December 9 foreign-subsidies tightening (Section 3) is the clearest European-institutional story of the day — unilateral economic statecraft built specifically to reduce China dependency, independent of any US tariff action. Nordic Compass — a bottom-up capital-markets and competitiveness initiative backed by Aker, DNB, EQT, Nasdaq, Nokia, Saab and Orsted, explicitly framed by participants as filling the gap left by the EU's own stalled capital-markets-union debate — is a genuine, if modest, structural development, complicated by the fact that Danske Bank and Swedbank are conspicuously not involved, "hinting at the type of concerns and turf wars that regularly derail attempts at pan-European reform." On defence and energy, the EU's fresh €45bn Ukraine financing loan (with the UK and Japan contributing further) is explicitly positioned as a counterweight to the Shenzhen summit, while Iceye's bid for Avio (Section 4) represents a rare concrete step in European defence-adjacent industrial consolidation. Separately, the UK's sanctions on companies building West Bank settlements directly triggered Israel's attorney-general freezing the contentious E1 settlement project — a clear instance of post-Brexit UK foreign policy exercising independent transatlantic-relevant leverage.
