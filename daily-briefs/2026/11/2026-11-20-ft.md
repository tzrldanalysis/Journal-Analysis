## 3. Geopolitics & Policy

**Trade/tariffs/sanctions mechanics.** The US Bureau of Industry and Security tightened export-control enforcement this week by scrapping the "independent packaging and testing" exemption Chinese chip designers used to source advanced TSMC/Samsung-made chips without direct BIS licensing — effective immediately, forcing both chipmakers to halt shipments to Chinese customers (including Baidu's Kunlun AI unit and ByteDance) pending new licence applications. Market implication: this squeezes Chinese AI-infrastructure buildout further just as Beijing is separately voicing trade-tension complaints about EU carbon border levies and EV tariffs (up to 35.3%) at COP31. Geopolitical signal: Washington is closing enforcement loopholes faster than China can route around them, which raises the probability of outright retaliation (rare-earth leverage, already visible in the Kenya Mrima Hill story below) rather than quiet workarounds.

Separately, the EU-China "safeguard" deal on EV/hybrid imports — effectively a voluntary export restraint dressed up to dodge WTO rules, per Alan Beattie's Opinion column — is the first major instance of Brussels openly adopting 1980s-style "managed trade." The mechanism: emergency import restrictions on hybrids, paired with two forthcoming tools (Industrial Accelerator Act, DiversifyEU) meant to build EU supply-chain resilience against China. Market implication: temporary breathing room for European carmakers already struggling against Chinese EV competition; geopolitical signal: Brussels has crossed a line it spent decades refusing to cross, and the US's own 1980s VER-on-Japan precedent (lifted in 1994, not 1984 as planned) is the explicit cautionary case cited — these arrangements have a strong tendency to outlive their stated purpose.

**Institutional/structural dynamics.** The Shenzhen trilateral's joint call to reform the UN and rein in the ICC is a direct structural assault on the postwar multilateral architecture, delivered the same week Rebeca Grynspan (UN secretary-general frontrunner) is publicly describing the UN as "arrogant" and "invisible" in the places that matter. ## 6. Prior Under Pressure

**Prior:** US bank regulation is on a steady deregulatory glide path under the current administration, and that's an unambiguous net positive for US bank equities relative to over-regulated European peers.

**Pressure:** Angeloni's piece complicates the "deregulation = good for banks" framing on its own terms — he argues the *timing* is the problem: capital requirements are being eased for US banks precisely when "the economy marches on, fuelled by a boom in AI-driven investment," meaning the deregulation is arriving at a cyclical peak rather than a trough, which is exactly the conditions under which loosened capital buffers get tested hardest. Combined with the PCAOB cutting inspection intensity in the same week, the signal is that US oversight capacity is contracting across bank supervision and audit quality simultaneously, right as leveraged AI-infrastructure financing (CoreWeave, private credit, synthetic securitization) is scaling. The "deregulation is good for US banks" prior survives in the short run but is harder to hold with full confidence as a multi-year bet.

## 7. What Would Have To Be True

**Implication 1:** Anthropic's $1.5tn valuation justifies itself over the medium term.
**Assumption:** Anthropic must successfully transition from a model-layer business (low switching costs, price-war exposure, 80% of revenue from six-month-old products) into Richard Waters's prescribed "full enterprise technology platform" — building its own infrastructure, a direct sales force, and defensible application-layer products — before price competition from open-weights models and vertically-integrated rivals compresses margins.
**Fragility:** If the "managed decline in pricing" Waters describes is *not* matched by equivalent declines in Anthropic's own compute costs (which depend on capacity-constrained chip supply now further squeezed by TSMC/Samsung's China shipment halt and Nvidia's own governance disruption), the moat never forms and the model-layer commoditization scenario the safety critics and Lex both independently flag becomes the base case.

**Implication 2:** The Shenzhen "Yalta 2.0" trilateral meaningfully advances nuclear arms-control dialogue.
**Assumption:** China must be willing to discuss reductions before achieving the warhead parity with the US/Russia that CIA veteran Dennis Wilder says is its actual precondition goal.
**Fragility:** Any US move to begin substantive three-way arms-control talks before parity is reached removes China's stated incentive to engage seriously — meaning the "groundbreaking" framing is almost certainly front-running a process that has no credible near-term mechanism to produce real reductions, and the UN-reform/ICC language in the joint statement is doing more diplomatic work (constraining courts, not warheads) than the nuclear rhetoric that led the coverage.
