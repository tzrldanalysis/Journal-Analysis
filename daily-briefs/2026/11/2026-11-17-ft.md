## 1. Today's Themes

**AI-finance stress has moved from equity sentiment into credit and governance structures.** Nvidia's board has rebuffed Trump's suggestion to reinstate Jensen Huang despite the chip chief's ouster over the Alice Huang affair; the stock is down more than 35% from its October peak and reports results today with no permanent CEO. Simultaneously: the ten biggest 2026 US IPOs have lost a fifth of their value on average ahead of Anthropic's roadshow (pricing $70–$78/share, up to $1.4tn valuation); Trump Media faces a November 30 deadline to repay ~$1bn in deeply out-of-the-money converts backed by a bitcoin treasury; and European banks are leaning on a record $35bn of significant risk transfers that the EBA and IMF now flag for "circles of risk" and forced-deleveraging potential. These are four separate stories in four separate sections of the paper, but they share one driver: capital formed around the AI boom is being tested simultaneously in equity, credit and governance terms.

**Great-power theatre in Shenzhen is substantively thinner than its "Yalta 2.0" billing.** [FT ANGLE] The FT's own Big Read and Gideon Rachman's column actively rebut the framing that dominates wire coverage of this week's Trump-Xi-Putin trilateral: China lacks a Marshall Plan equivalent, has invested little in global public goods relative to its rhetoric, and remains passive on both the Ukraine and Iran conflicts despite claiming global-governance leadership. The analytical conclusion — Beijing wants "power without responsibility" rather than genuine hegemonic capacity — is a structural read a US outlet is less likely to foreground over the photo-op symbolism itself.

**EU institutional unity on Ukraine financing is fraying along a fault line that now threatens the bloc's own budget negotiation.** The Commission's proposed €45bn loan against the shared EU budget — identical in mechanism to last year's €90bn facility — is opposed by the Czech Republic, Slovakia, Hungary and Bulgaria. Chancellor Merz has explicitly linked this to the unrelated 2028–34 shared budget talks, meaning a stalemate on one now contaminates the other ahead of the November 26–27 Brussels summit. This lands the same day Ukraine launched its largest drone attack of the war on the Crimean Bridge — a reminder that financing fatigue is compounding at the point of maximum battlefield intensity, not easing into a diplomatic resolution.

**Developed-market labour immobility is now a cross-central-bank concern, and it dovetails with Japan's surprise contraction.** Fed governor Waller, BoE deputy governor Lombardelli and the ECB have each separately flagged a "low hire, low fire" equilibrium that looks resilient on unemployment data but is quietly eroding productivity and wage dynamism — a reading reinforced by Japan's unexpected 0.1% annualised GDP contraction, where consumption fell despite wage gains, undermining the BoJ's rate-normalisation narrative.

**[WEAK SIGNAL] Balance-sheet engineering is substituting for fresh capital across sovereign, corporate and bank risk transfer simultaneously.** No single story names this, but the pattern recurs: the EU's Ukraine loan is borrowed against its own budget rather than raised fresh; New World Development's HK$24.9bn rights issue is priced at an 80% discount specifically to let offshore lenders extend HK$82bn of maturities by two years for a 10% cash sweetener rather than a real repayment; Trump Media is weighing refinancing converts "at a high interest rate" rather than raising equity it has "all but ruled out"; and the SRT market exists precisely to let banks generate regulatory capital relief without raising fresh equity. None of these is framed as connected in today's edition, but taken together they describe a financial system extending and restructuring obligations rather than funding them.

## 2. Finance & Markets

**Rates.** US 10-year sits at 5.13%, 30-year 5.48%, both essentially flat on the day (+0.00–0.01pp) even as the Nasdaq 100 fell over 2%. UK gilts (10yr 5.36%) and Bunds (10yr 3.38%) show the same pattern — no flight-to-quality bid accompanying the equity selloff. That absence is itself a signal: this looks like an AI-specific equity rotation rather than a broad risk-off impulse, consistent with a market pricing an idiosyncratic Nvidia/IPO-froth story rather than a macro shock.

**Credit.** European banks are set to issue a record $35bn of significant risk transfers (SRTs) this year, per S&P Global Ratings, as banks increasingly use the instruments to "bolster returns on capital" rather than purely manage balance-sheet growth — a shift in motivation S&P explicitly flags as the risk factor behind potential "forced deleveraging" if investor demand for SRTs dries up. The EBA has separately warned of "circles of risk" from banks financing the same credit funds that buy their SRT paper, and the IMF has flagged banks providing leverage for credit funds to buy credit-linked notes issued by *other* banks — a closed loop that concentrates risk exactly where it is designed to be dispersed. Santander, BNP Paribas and Barclays are the largest SRT issuers. Separately, a reader letter responding to the FT's November 12 report on banks hedging AI data-centre loan exposure via credit insurance argues the hedge is weaker than portrayed: specific-risk underwriting for data centres would be hard to obtain and expensive, and most credit-insurance cover runs under one year against financing with far longer tenors — a genuine duration mismatch in the AI-capex credit chain that the market may be underpricing.

## 3. Geopolitics & Policy

**Trade, tariffs and sanctions mechanics.**

The US tightened AI chip export controls on Sunday, introducing new licensing requirements for shipments to overseas data centres serving Chinese customers at scale, and for advanced-chip exports to countries with "a higher-than-normal risk of diversion" — explicitly naming Singapore, the jurisdiction of Megaspeed, the company at the centre of the Huang-affair export-control controversy. Mechanism: closes the specific loophole Huang is alleged to have helped Megaspeed exploit; further rules tracking majority-owned subsidiaries of blacklisted firms are still pending. Market implication: reinforces enforcement risk for any China-exposed chip revenue (relevant to Nvidia's reporting today). Geopolitical signal: Washington is tightening the technology-containment track in parallel with, not instead of, this week's conciliatory Trump-Xi summit diplomacy — a bifurcation the FT View editorial explicitly endorses as "controlled access, not blockade" rather than full decoupling. Mechanism: no tariff, but an economic-security alignment framework analogous in intent to a tech-era alliance structure. Market implication: entrenches "friend-shoring" of chip/mineral supply chains, favouring allied-jurisdiction suppliers over Chinese ones. ## 6. Prior Under Pressure

**Prior:** Low, stable unemployment is sufficient evidence of a healthy, dynamic labour market in developed economies.

**Pressure:** Fed governor Waller, BoE deputy governor Lombardelli and the ECB have each independently flagged a "low hire, low fire" equilibrium — hiring, resignation and job-switching rates stuck near decade lows in the US, and UK job-switching not far above global-financial-crisis lows — as a loss of labour-market "dynamism" that coexists with resilient activity and low unemployment, which Waller explicitly calls "unusual." Claudia Sahm and Fabien Postel-Vinay separately argue this reflects a structural mismatch (vacancies concentrated in sectors like healthcare that don't match available skills) rather than cyclical health. Japan's surprise 0.1% annualised GDP contraction — driven by a 0.3% consumption drop despite rising wages — independently reinforces the pattern: low unemployment and wage growth are no longer reliably translating into consumer or productivity strength across three major developed economies simultaneously.
