## 1. Today's Themes

**Divided government is being re-priced from political risk into a credit put, but the mechanism doesn't actually exist yet.** Pictet's Arun Sai called the Democratic sweep "good for markets" because "legislative gridlock is good for markets," and the tape agreed: S&P 500 +1.8%, Nasdaq 100 +1.6%, Brent -8.7% to $94.60, UK gilt yields -13bp (the biggest one-day drop since May) to 5.16%, French 10-year yields -16bp to 4.78%. The trade rests on the assumption that a Democrat-controlled Congress constrains Trump's Iran war. Katie Martin's Markets Insight column flags the hole in that thesis directly: "the White House keeps its discretionary powers over strikes, blockades and sanctions licences." Congress can raise the *political cost* of continuing the war; it cannot, on its own, stop it.

**The AI capex supercycle is colliding head-on with Democrat-controlled physical infrastructure.** Goldman Sachs estimates Democratic data-centre moratoria could hit 45% of planned US capacity, and Lex notes the five states leading data-centre construction are "all now governed by Democrats with a hawkish bent." The market reaction split cleanly along a capital-structure line: hyperscalers who already own built capacity rallied (Amazon +7.1%, Microsoft +7%, Alphabet +4.5%) while financing-dependent "neoclouds" were hit (CoreWeave -8.7%, Nebius -5.3%) alongside chip suppliers (Micron -5.4%). This connects directly to Anthropic's IPO filing disclosing $900bn of cumulative committed compute spending and to Bill Gates's warning, at the FT's Future of AI summit, that AI-enabled bioweapon risk is being left unregulated by "White House inaction."

**Washington's willingness to use extraterritorial leverage against institutions and counterparties is tightening, and the pushback is now structural rather than rhetorical.** Three separate stories share this spine: the ICC's registrar publicly asking Brussels to activate its sanctions "blocking statute" against US measures; Oracle cancelling a $7bn, five-year AI-chip lease with Tencent after direct White House pressure; and Nvidia telling cloud clients they must sell at least 51% of their services to non-Chinese buyers. [FT ANGLE] Each is covered here as a test of institutional sovereignty — European or Chinese — rather than as a US domestic-policy story, which is precisely the frame a US-primacy outlet would de-emphasise.

**Opaque internal accounting is a recurring, not isolated, failure mode this cycle.** Heraeus systematically understated precious-metals content for 450 customers over a decade in a practice its own lawyers called "legally highly questionable," Accenture published an AI research paper riddled with fabricated citations; Anthropic's IPO filing excludes $8.2bn of accumulated stock-based compensation from its reported operating loss, a near-exact echo of Uber's 2019 IPO, whose shares fell once the deferred $3.6bn hit the P&L. [WEAK SIGNAL] None of these stories reference each other, but they describe the same underlying failure — insiders controlling which number the outside world is permitted to see — surfacing simultaneously across a refiner, a consultancy, a law firm's own economics (see the Letters page critique of Weil Gotshal's billing model) and a $350bn-plus IPO.

**Credit stress is migrating from single distressed names into interconnected, crowded hedge-fund books.** Arini Capital's $7bn flagship fund is down more than 26% this year, facing margin calls from its prime-broker banks, with "everyone... shorting his positions" according to a rival manager. Its exposures — Brightspeed (an Apollo-backed telecom now preparing for Chapter 11), the entirety of a £598mn Very Group bond issue, Merlin Entertainments, Claritev and Sabre — sit inside a web of restructuring groups and CDS positions that other funds also hold, turning one manager's forced unwind into a market-wide mark-to-market event.

## 2. Finance & Markets

**Rates.** Eurozone flash inflation hit a three-year high of 4.0% in October (up from 3.8%), with energy inflation at 20.8% — the highest since January 2023 — while core held at 2.5%. ECB officials are now pricing a December hike as near-certain, with Pantheon Macroeconomics calling for another in March; Carsten Brzeski at ING called the figures a hit that "will continue in the coming months" as cheaper diesel is offset by pricier gas and power. Against that backdrop, global government bonds nonetheless rallied hard on the midterm result: US 10-year yields -5bp to 4.89%, UK gilts -13bp to 5.16% (the sharpest daily move since May), French 10-years -16bp to 4.78%. The two moves are in tension — a market betting simultaneously on sticky European energy-driven inflation and on US political change easing the same energy shock. France's 10-year yield of 4.79% carries a 152bp spread over German Bunds (3.27%), still materially above the sub-5% levels seen before French yields breached 5% last month for the first time since 2002.

**Credit.** Arini's unwind is the standout credit story. Rival traders estimate it sold more than $2bn face value of positions in chaotic trading over the past week; Brightspeed, where Arini holds a roughly $2bn position concentrated in the riskiest tiers of debt, has begun planning for a potential Chapter 11 according to the Wall Street Journal. Separately, Weil Gotshal's wind-down is converting a law-firm collapse into a counterparty event: up to 120 lawyers are in talks to move to Gibson Dunn, and former partners have hired outside litigators to fight for stakes that could otherwise be wiped out in the shutdown — a direct parallel to how Dewey LeBoeuf's 2012 bankruptcy created years of recovery litigation.

**FX.** The dollar fell 0.2% against a basket as investors "questioned whether the boost to the economy from AI could weaken" given the political shift, per Barclays' Lefteris Farmakis. The euro was essentially unmoved by the inflation surprise, up only 0.2% to $1.122 — a sign the market sees the ECB as constrained rather than newly hawkish.

**Commodities.** Brent crashed 8.7% to $94.60 — a two-month low and down roughly 17% since the end of last week — on bets that the midterm result increases pressure on Trump to settle with Iran; WTI fell 8% to about $88.40, down more than 15% on the week. Natixis's Joel Hancock warned that, absent an actual peace deal, Brent is likely to move back above $100 given the continued difficulty of shipping crude out of the Gulf — meaning today's rally is a bet on an outcome, not a resolved fact. Copper tells the inverse story to oil: the World Bank now expects $13,000/tonne next year, 18% above its April forecast, on AI-infrastructure demand and structurally limited mine supply. That boom is bifurcating along the value chain — Zijin Mining's gross margin on copper concentrate rose from 65% to 71% year-on-year, while smelters are being squeezed by treatment charges that have fallen to zero or turned negative, a genuine credit-relevant divergence between miners and the processors that depend on them.

**Equities.** Divided government drove the headline rally, but positioning beneath the index was the real story: hyperscalers up sharply (Amazon +7.1%, Microsoft +7%, Alphabet +4.5%) on the logic that "Amazon already has data centres, they've already made their investments," freeing cash for debt service or buybacks, against "neoclouds" CoreWeave (-8.7%) and Nebius (-5.3%), whose business models depend on financing *new* capacity into a moratorium wave. Defence stocks fell on fears a Democrat Congress reverses Pete Hegseth's push to lift the defence budget toward $1.5tn: Lockheed Martin and L3Harris both fell more than 6%, Northrop Grumman about 4%. In China, "national team" state-linked ETF buying hit $11.5bn on Tuesday — the second-highest daily figure on record — concentrated unusually in tech: Shanghai's Star 50 jumped 9.9% and Shenzhen's ChiNext 6.4%, against a broader CSI 300 gain of 2.3%. BNP Paribas's Song Zhe called it "a regime shift" toward specifically defending tech-sub-index valuations ahead of CXMT's Shanghai listing — a signal Beijing now treats its AI-adjacent equity complex as strategically distinct from the broader market. Lex's framing is worth folding in directly: delayed AI capex is, in net-present-value terms, cheaper capex — "$1tn spent next year is like spending $870bn today" at a 15% discount rate — which argues the hyperscaler rally on bad regulatory news was not irrational.

## 3. Geopolitics & Policy

**Trade, tariffs and sanctions mechanics.** The US sanctions regime against the International Criminal Court, announced last month, gave firms 180 days to sever ties with the Hague tribunal; Axa has already dropped the ICC as a client over the threat. ICC registrar Osvaldo Zavala Giler is now explicitly asking the EU to activate its "blocking statute" — a mechanism allowing European companies to disregard US extraterritorial sanctions — calling it "an instrument that is reasserting European sovereignty." The European Commission, by contrast, is offering only "political reassurances" while it "continues to pursue diplomatic outreach," refusing to act before the sanctions take effect. Market implication: compliance desks at European banks and insurers now face a binary choice once the 180-day window closes — US correspondent-banking access or EU blocking-statute compliance — with no clarity yet on which Brussels will actually enforce. [FT ANGLE] Geopolitical signal: this is Trump's declared campaign to dismantle what he calls an "anti-American tribunal," and the FT treats the EU's hesitation as a live test of whether European "sovereignty" rhetoric has operational teeth.

A second, sharper mechanism: Oracle cancelled a $7bn, five-year lease giving Tencent access to roughly 100,000 advanced AI chips across south-east Asian data centres, after US officials "raised concerns" directly with Oracle and Guangdong officials separately pressured Tencent to favour domestic chips. Nvidia has gone further, telling cloud clients they must now sell at least 51% of their services to non-Chinese companies. Market implication: US cloud and compute providers with Chinese enterprise exposure — even routed through third countries — now carry unpriced political-cancellation risk on long-dated contracts. Geopolitical signal: both Washington and Beijing are independently discouraging the same cross-border AI-compute relationships, which argues for faster, not slower, US-China tech decoupling regardless of who is "winning" the broader trade relationship.

**Institutional and structural dynamics.** European leaders greeted the Democratic sweep as a chance to "moderate fractious transatlantic relations" — Spain's Pedro Sánchez called it the "crumbling" of a "far-right wave," Germany's Boris Pistorius pledged to keep working "closely and in a spirit of trust" with Washington on Ukraine. Trump's own Nato ambassador, Matthew Whitaker, pushed back hard: "the foreign policy of the United States has not changed. And we shouldn't expect that it would change in a midterm election." [FT ANGLE] The FT gives equal weight to both readings, a balance a US-primacy outlet would be less likely to strike.

South Korea's drift toward Moscow is straining the US-aligned bloc from an unexpected angle: Seoul shipped 176,000 tonnes of diesel to Russia in July and August (including via tankers under UK and EU sanctions), recalled its ambassador to Kyiv for the first time since 2008 over a prisoner-transfer dispute, and a Seoul-based European diplomat told the FT flatly that South Korea "aren't really 'like-minded'" anymore. The structural stakes for Europe are concrete: Poland alone has ordered more than $18bn of Korean tanks, howitzers, fighter jets and rocket launchers since 2022 to replenish stockpiles drawn down for Ukraine, meaning European defence-industrial resilience is now partly hostage to a Seoul-Moscow relationship Brussels cannot influence. France, meanwhile, doubled its Russian LNG imports to €446mn in September — prompting one Brussels-based analyst to note flatly that "Europe doesn't have a unified position towards Russia."

Ukraine's EU-accession track is now explicitly diverging from Moldova's: Brussels says Chisinau is on track to close membership talks by early 2028 while Kyiv needs "another year" and an "acceleration of the pace of reforms" on anti-corruption, even as Ukraine faces a €70bn financing gap for next year, two-thirds of which the Commission has vowed to cover, potentially through joint debt. Zelenskyy has called decoupling the two tracks "morally wrong."

## 4. Business & Companies

CVS Health's break-up of its $117bn healthcare empire — spinning off a 9,000-store, roughly $420bn-revenue retail pharmacy business from the Caremark PBM and Aetna insurance units — is the kind of move that normally draws an approving market reaction. Instead CVS shares fell 11% in early trading. The disconnect matters: it suggests the market doubts the standalone pharmacy unit's economics just as Arkansas and Tennessee move to ban PBM-pharmacy co-ownership outright (effective 2028) and bipartisan Warren-Hawley legislation targets the same structure in the Senate — the break-up looks like a regulatory pre-emption rather than a value-creation story, and credit investors in the to-be-spun pharmacy entity should treat the "sell the news" reaction as a signal, not noise.

BMW delivered its second profit warning since June, cutting its core automotive margin guidance to a range of -0.5% to +0.5% from an earlier 1-3%, after China sales fell a quarter year-on-year and combustion-engine market share there dropped below 35%. Oddly, BMW shares rose 5.5% on the news — Lex-grade evidence that the market had already discounted worse, or is rewarding the simultaneous announcement of 8,000 back-office job cuts (5% of the workforce) plus more than 100 additional management-role cuts. Chief financial officer Walter Mertl still insists combustion engines will "be alive" in China "up to the next decade," a forecast that sits uneasily against the group's own sales data.

Oracle's cancellation of its $7bn Tencent chip-leasing deal (cross-referenced in Section 3) removes Tencent's largest overseas compute-leasing arrangement and signals that even SE Asia-routed US cloud exposure to Chinese AI developers is no longer politically tenable — a direct hit to the "neutral infrastructure provider" positioning Oracle, and peers like Nvidia, had been trying to maintain.

Weil, Gotshal & Manges' wind-down is both a legal-industry and a counterparty story: a near-40-strong funds team is moving to Gibson Dunn in London in one of the largest lateral moves in legal-industry history, while roughly 10 former partners have hired litigator Peter Wang to fight for stakes that could otherwise be "wiped out" once office-lease and vendor liabilities are netted against client receivables. A letter on today's Opinion page from a Yale law professor argues the real disease isn't rainmaker pay but "too many ordinary partners... paid far too much" under an hourly-billing model unable to survive genuine competition — a structural read on why Weil's collapse may not be the last.

Ahold Delhaize cut its full-year forecast after a cyberattack reduced expected net sales by €200-250mn and operating profit by €75-100mn in the fourth quarter, part of a wave of unattributed attacks that also hit France's national blood service and freezer systems at dozens of European supermarkets within weeks — some researchers suggest a Russia link, though nobody has claimed responsibility. **AD (Ahold Delhaize)** — guidance cut on a cyberattack whose repeated, cross-border pattern across European food infrastructure looks more like sustained targeting than opportunism.

Uber's Q3 operating profit of $1.4bn missed the $2.2bn analyst expectation after a $484mn legal/tax reserve, with chief executive Dara Khosrowshahi explicitly blaming Middle East war-driven fuel costs — a rare direct, quantified linkage between the Iran conflict and a US consumer-tech earnings miss. Uber is cutting roughly 3,300 roles (a tenth of its global workforce) to fund a robotaxi build-out it has committed more than $10bn to, targeting 2,000-plus autonomous vehicles in 2027 and 120,000 over time.

Heraeus's precious-metals scandal widened: an independent probe found the refiner systematically understated metal content for 450 customers over a decade in a practice its own investigating law firm called "legally highly questionable," pushing total provisions to €300mn and implicating 16 current and former employees — including former chief executive Jan Rinnert — in an ongoing criminal fraud investigation.

## 5. Steel-Man

**Consensus view:** Divided US government is unambiguously bullish for risk assets because it constrains an erratic president and removes policy-shock tail risk — hence today's simultaneous equity rally, bond rally and oil collapse.

**Steel-man:** The FT's own reporting undercuts this. Katie Martin's column is explicit that Trump "keeps his discretionary powers over strikes, blockades and sanctions licences" — Congress has raised the political cost of the Iran war, not removed the legal capacity to continue it, and Monday's strikes on Qeshm Island happened without congressional authorisation in the first place. The FT View itself flags two unresolved risks: a "vituperative president" wielding veto power against everything Congress passes (Trump has pledged exactly that) and Democrats drifting left or over-reaching on impeachment, either of which produces pure gridlock without the moderating benefits markets are pricing today. Stephen Roach's column goes further, arguing the relief rally is itself a symptom of late-cycle excess rather than a genuine fundamentals repricing — "every bubble has its final Fomo phase" — meaning the rally reflects an election outcome, not resolved inflation, resolved AI-financing risk or a resolved war. Most pointedly, Japanese trading-house executives — commercially exposed to exactly this question through oil and copper positioning — are hedging between a "lame duck" and an "angry duck" Trump, explicitly pricing the possibility that a legislatively boxed-in president lashes out through foreign policy rather than being restrained by it.

## 6. Prior Under Pressure

**Prior:** Trump's tariff programme, whatever its sectoral costs, has not meaningfully damaged the broader growth and markets narrative — tariffs are noise against a backdrop of AI-driven growth and a resilient "Trump put."

**Pressure:** Michael Strain's Opinion piece supplies a direct empirical rebuttal from inside today's paper: manufacturing's share of US employment fell from 8.5% to 8% since Trump's current term began, the goods trade deficit has nearly doubled since January 2017 (up 55% in the past year alone), and the policy's own goals — more manufacturing jobs, a smaller trade deficit — are "wrong-headed" and "neither has been met," in Strain's words, while the 30-year mortgage rate pushed above 7% on the back of a $2tn 2026 budget deficit, the largest outside war or recession in US history. Exit polls show nearly two-thirds of midterm voters now believe the tariffs hurt the economy, and that same electorate has just installed a Congress with every incentive to treat further deficit-financed stimulus as politically toxic rather than automatic — the fiscal backdrop that has underwritten market confidence in "growth outrunning the tariffs" is now actively contested, not assumed.

## 7. What Would Have To Be True

**Implication:** A Democrat-controlled Congress durably constrains Trump's Iran policy, sustaining today's oil and bond rally.
**Assumption:** Political cost (subpoenas, appropriations leverage, investigative pressure) must translate into actual operational restraint on strikes, blockades and sanctions licensing — powers that remain purely executive.
**Fragility:** A single unilateral strike or de-escalation announcement reverses the entire trade instantly; Natixis already flags that, absent an actual peace deal, Brent likely moves back above $100 purely on continued Strait-of-Hormuz shipping constraints that have nothing to do with Congress.

**Implication:** Hyperscalers (Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet) are structurally insulated from the Democrat-led data-centre backlash, while neoclouds and chip suppliers absorb the regulatory risk.
**Assumption:** The 45% of planned US data-centre capacity Goldman Sachs flags as exposed to moratoria must sit predominantly in third-party and neocloud buildouts, with existing hyperscaler-owned sites exempt from retroactive curtailment on power allocation or water use.
**Fragility:** Lex notes the five leading data-centre-building states are now all governed by Democrats "with a hawkish bent" — if moratorium logic extends from blocking new permits to constraining the grid draw of existing hyperscaler sites, the entire long-hyperscaler/short-neocloud trade reverses.

## 11. Cross-Publication Delta

A WSJ treatment of the midterms and the Iran war would almost certainly center on US conservative-movement soul-searching (its own editorial board, quoted in today's FT, already called Trump the "main architect" of the defeat) and Fed-credibility framing; the FT instead foregrounds the European diplomatic "reset" angle — Sánchez, Merz, Pistorius — and South Korea's structural drift toward Moscow, stories a US-primacy lens would likely skip entirely.

The FT connects the same story to the UK's own data-centre and founders'-relief tax policy (Lex), Qatar's Gulf LNG rerouting and China's "national team" tech-stock defense, giving the US data-centre story an explicitly global capital-flows frame that a domestic-earnings lens would omit.

The FT gives the ICC registrar's blocking-statute request direct, sympathetic voice and treats it as a test of EU sovereignty — a regulatory and structural framing distinctly European in character.

## 12. One To Watch

The ICC "blocking statute" request.
