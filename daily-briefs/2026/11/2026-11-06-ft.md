## 1. Today's Themes

**The AI data-centre backlash has graduated from NIMBY nuisance to bipartisan policy risk, and the market is already pricing it.** Voters in Texas, Georgia and Ohio elected anti-data-centre governors, and 38 local ballot measures restricting data-centre development passed this week. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell as much as 11.9% intraday — more than 38% below its October high and into bear-market territory — while Nvidia shed over $400bn in value at one point and lost the "world's most valuable company" crown to Apple this same week. AI executives at the FT's own Future of AI summit conceded they had fuelled the backlash themselves ("we have spent two years pretty dangerously telling people that AI will change the future of work for the worse," said Niantic's John Hanke). Cross-reference: this directly threatens Nscale's $35bn IPO (Business & Companies) and is the backdrop to CoreWeave's 25% slide (Counterparty & Institutional Risk).

**JD Vance's public break with Trump over the Iran war turns a talking point into a structural fracture inside the Republican coalition.** "The reason we lost is the war," Vance wrote on X, reposted by Donald Trump Jr — an unmistakable 2028 positioning move. The Michigan Senate flip to Abdul El-Sayed, explicitly built on tying living costs to Iran-war spending and Israel policy, and a Pew finding that 62% of Americans (and a majority of under-39 Republicans) now view Israel's government unfavourably, both support a genuine partisan realignment rather than a one-cycle protest vote.

**Capital is rotating out of US AI-exposed equities into Europe, and the data confirms it rather than just the narrative.** The Stoxx Europe 600 is up 10.7% year-to-date against 9.4% for the S&P 500, Germany's Dax hit a fresh record, and strategists at Société Générale, Jefferies and State Street all independently described the same mechanism: money moving out of chipmakers and emerging-market Asia into non-AI-exposed European equities and bonds. Cross-reference: this sits alongside a renewables-sector rally (Ørsted +12% this week) tied to the same "regulatory clarity from a weakened White House" logic.

**[FT ANGLE] Europe is already discounting a merely diminished Trump presidency rather than a reversal, which is a different trade than Wall Street is making.** Robert Shrimsley's column is explicit: "the loss of a bad America is not the same as the return of the old one." Giorgia Meloni's broadside at EU leaders over "disjointed, unco-ordinated" Ukraine diplomacy, landing in the same news cycle, reinforces that European capitals see structural American disengagement, not a correction, as the baseline to plan around.

**China is now willing to use its industrial-approval authority against Japanese and Western AI-adjacent capital just as aggressively as Washington is trying to re-shore critical minerals — a pattern, not an isolated veto.** Beijing's NDRC blocked SoftBank's $5.4bn purchase of ABB's robotics division over fears that Chinese robotics data could be used to train AI models including OpenAI's. This echoes Beijing's earlier demand that Meta unwind its purchase of Singapore's Manus. Cross-reference: this runs in parallel, on the same day, to a US feature on the lobbying boom around Trump's critical-minerals re-shoring push — both capitals are weaponising industrial policy simultaneously.

## 2. Finance & Markets

**Rates.** The 10-year Treasury yield fell 6bp to 4.84% and the 2-year fell 8bp to 4.50% as the Nasdaq rout drove a flight to havens. The Bank of England raised rates to 4% — its first hike since 2023 — and signalled at least one further increase "as soon as next month," yet 10-year gilts barely moved (5.114%, "little changed"), a sign the move was fully priced rather than a surprise tightening. The more consequential rates story is in France: the 10-year OAT yield, which crossed 5% for the first time since 2002 last month, eased to 4.78% as European bond markets "breathe a sigh of relief" on the back of lower energy prices and falling US yields — a relief rally built on the midterm result and fading Iran-war oil-price risk, not on any improvement in France's own fiscal trajectory.

**Credit.** UBS cut spending on external consultants and contractors by $825mn this year and has shed more than 5,500 jobs (21,500 since the Credit Suisse rescue), evidence the integration is now mechanically complete even as a parliamentary vote on tougher capital requirements remains a live tail risk for the stock. Maersk — the clean read on global trade volumes — raised its full-year operating-profit guidance for the third time this year, to $8bn–$8.5bn, yet shares fell 5% after third-quarter Ebitda of $4.7bn missed the Visible Alpha consensus of $5.5bn; the gap between a confident full-year guide and a quarterly miss is the kind of divergence credit desks should be watching into the Christmas peak-shipping season.

**FX.** The euro rose 0.3% to $1.122 against the dollar, consistent with the broader rotation out of dollar-denominated AI exposure.

**Commodities.** Brent rebounded 1.45% to $96.82 and WTI gained 2.35% to $90.19, recovering part of Wednesday's near-9% plunge that came on hopes the scale of the Republican defeat would pressure Trump to end the Iran war — a direct, same-day transmission mechanism from US domestic politics to the oil complex. Gold continued grinding higher, up 1.26% to $4,118.10.

**Equities.** The Nasdaq 100 fell as much as 6.4% intraday (closing down 4.36%) in its worst one-day move since the Covid pandemic; the S&P 500 closed down 3.83% to 7,394.44. Nvidia fell as much as 9% intraday; Tesla and SpaceX (both Musk-controlled) fell 9% and 7% respectively, each piling onto prior-day losses. CoreWeave, flagged separately below, was off 25%. European indices simultaneously rallied — the Stoxx 600 rose 1.8% and the Dax touched a record high — confirming this is a sector rotation out of US data-centre capex exposure into "everything else across the globe," in the words of Deutsche Bank's Jim Reid, rather than a broad risk-off event.

**The SoftBank-ABB angle (this edition's Lex-equivalent, Richard Waters' Business Insight column).** China's block "renders the acquisition virtually pointless" if China exposure is excluded, since over 90% of ABB's robotics unit's output goes to Chinese customers; SoftBank's original offer implied 19.4x Ebitda versus lower peer multiples, meaning any restructured deal or spin-off alternative would likely come at a materially lower valuation. ABB itself "comes out even worse": it will now likely have to fund its £4.1bn Rotork acquisition from its own balance sheet rather than from robotics-unit sale proceeds — a specific, quantifiable cross-border M&A credit event hiding inside a geopolitics story.

## 3. Geopolitics & Policy

**Trade, tariffs and sanctions mechanics.** China's National Development and Reform Commission has formally prohibited SoftBank's $5.4bn acquisition of ABB's China robotics operations, citing concerns that robotics data could be transferred overseas to train AI models (including OpenAI's). The mechanism is a straightforward national-security review veto, not a negotiated modification — SoftBank is now assessing whether it can even reapply. This is the same lever Beijing used against Meta's purchase of Manus, confirming a repeatable Chinese playbook for severing foreign AI-adjacent capital from Chinese data and manufacturing exactly as the US pursues its own critical-minerals re-shoring (see Business & Companies). Separately, US sanctions-evasion mechanics on Russian diesel are visibly fraying: two of the first three tankers carrying Russian diesel under Trump's pre-midterm deal with Putin — the sanctioned Talisman and the Hong Kong-owned Leruo — have diverted away from the US Atlantic coast toward Brazil and the Mediterranean; New Jersey's governor has written to port operators threatening penalties on anyone unloading the cargoes. US distillate inventories have fallen below 100mn barrels for the first time in more than two decades, meaning the diesel squeeze the deal was meant to relieve is intensifying rather than easing.

**Institutional and structural dynamics.** A leaked Russian defence-industry production schedule, obtained by the FT via Ukrainian intelligence firm Dallas Analytics, shows Russia agreed to supply Iran with 90 APU-L missile-launcher systems (part of a wider order including Yak-130 jet trainers), directly contradicting Trump's claim that Moscow was doing "very little, if anything" to help Tehran. This is a credibility problem for any US-brokered Iran ceasefire premised on Russian disengagement. On the US side, the midterm result is producing what Vermont senator Peter Welch calls a new "consensus position of opposition and distrust" among Democrats toward Netanyahu's government — a structural shift in a relationship Welch says "has been sacrosanct in Washington, transcending party divisions" for generations. **[FT ANGLE]** Meloni's letter to European Council president Antonio Costa, accusing fellow EU leaders of a "disjointed, unco-ordinated" approach to Ukraine talks and warning the bloc risks becoming "irrelevant" in any peace process, lands at the precise moment the US is distracted by its own domestic realignment — raising the structural risk of a diplomatic vacuum just as Steve Witkoff's trilateral Abu Dhabi talks with Moscow and Kyiv are being arranged.

## 4. Business & Companies

**CVS** will spin off roughly 9,000 pharmacies into a new public company, unwinding two decades of healthcare-conglomerate integration under converging bipartisan pressure (Republican Josh Hawley and Democrat Elizabeth Warren both pushed PBM-breakup legislation this year; incoming Senate Finance chair Ron Wyden has pledged scrutiny). **CVS** fell 12.5% on the announcement despite management framing it as pre-emptive positioning rather than distress.

**SoftBank/ABB** — covered above; a $5.4bn deal blocked outright by Chinese regulators, with knock-on effects for ABB's own M&A funding (Rotork acquisition) and for SoftBank's broader "physical AI" strategy, which also spans 1X Technologies and Skild AI.

**Berlin is close to taking a 50% stake in Franco-German tank maker KNDS**, ending a long-running flotation saga, at a reported €11.2bn valuation plus a further €1bn "top-up" payment to the Wegmann family shareholders — a direct beneficiary of Europe's rearmament drive, though Rheinmetall (KNDS's Boxer co-producer) has nearly halved this year on Berlin's cancelled frigate programme and Boxer delays, illustrating how unevenly that rearmament capex is flowing even within the same supply chain.

**WB Electronics**, the Polish drone and loitering-munitions maker, is preparing a Warsaw IPO that could value it at €5bn–€7bn, with revenue up tenfold since 2021 to over $1bn and a $9bn order backlog — direct evidence of European defence-tech valuations diverging from the "traditional" defence contractors (Rheinmetall) toward unmanned-systems specialists.

**Nscale**, the UK's most valuable AI start-up, lost out on an expanded Microsoft data-centre leasing deal worth "tens of billions of dollars," jeopardising its $35bn IPO ambitions; the company posted a $1bn net loss on just $141mn of revenue in the first half of 2026 against a stated $103bn backlog — a financing-math problem that will recur across the neocloud sector (see Counterparty & Institutional Risk).

**Toyota** (full-year profit forecast raised to ¥3.9tn, buyback to ¥1.3tn) and **Sony** (profit outlook raised on a 45% operating-profit jump) both beat materially, a reminder that today's tech-sector pain is a specifically AI-infrastructure-financing story rather than a broad corporate-earnings one. **UBS** cut external advisory spending by $825mn and 5,500 jobs as Credit Suisse integration winds down. **Blue Origin** will not launch its New Glenn rocket again until January 2027 after a launchpad-rebuilding delay, falling further behind SpaceX, which has meanwhile completed a $10bn fundraising at a $140bn post-money valuation. **David Jones**, Australia's 188-year-old department-store chain (6,000 employees, 38 stores), fell into administration after failing to agree a landlord restructuring, with Hilco — its main secured creditor at over A$200mn exposure — extending a further A$10mn just to keep it trading through Christmas.

## 5. Steel-Man

**Consensus view:** The Democratic sweep marks a durable repudiation of Trump's Iran war and erratic tariff diplomacy, and signals a lasting US foreign-policy retrenchment that structurally benefits Europe and meaningfully constrains the AI infrastructure build-out.

**Steel-man:** Barry Eichengreen's own column in this edition makes the strongest case against this. A change in the partisan composition of Congress "will have little impact on a policymaking process driven by executive orders where Republican representatives are cowed by their president and Democrats offer no meaningful solutions." Congress could, in theory, reclaim tariff authority, pass a war-powers resolution on Iran, or legislate data-centre community-review requirements — but Trump would veto all three, and there is no override majority. The stock market's violent reaction may therefore be pricing a policy shift that cannot actually be legislated: AI infrastructure policy remains an executive-branch and state-level matter, and Trump "views America's position in the AI race as more important than community fears." If the steel-man holds, this week's rotation out of US tech and into Europe is a sentiment overreaction waiting to partially reverse once the market registers that Congress's practical leverage over Trump's foreign and industrial policy is far smaller than the vote share implies.

## 6. Prior Under Pressure

**Prior:** AI capital expenditure is a self-sustaining secular supercycle, largely insulated from political risk because voters neither understand nor care about data-centre siting, power demand or AI financing structures.

**Pressure:** 38 local ballot measures restricting data-centre development passed this week; voters in Texas, Georgia and Ohio specifically elected anti-data-centre governors; the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell into bear-market territory, 38% below its October high; and AI-company executives speaking at the FT's own Future of AI summit explicitly admitted they themselves fuelled the opposition through two years of messaging that "AI will change the future of work for the worse." The backlash is no longer a NIMBY sideshow — it has become, in the words of one executive, a genuine "political symbol," comparable to pandemic-era mask opposition, and it is now a stated priority for an incoming Democrat-controlled Congress.

**Implication:** The new Democratic Congress meaningfully constrains US support for Israel and reshapes Middle East risk premia.

## 12. One To Watch

China's NDRC blocking SoftBank's $5.4bn purchase of ABB's robotics division is a small deal buried on a business page beneath wall-to-wall midterm coverage, but it is the cleanest template yet for a pattern that will recur across every cross-border robotics, AI-hardware or data-centre deal with China exposure: Beijing using its approval authority to sever Japanese and Western AI-adjacent capital from Chinese data and manufacturing, exactly as Washington simultaneously tries to re-shore critical minerals through its own approval and subsidy machinery (see the lobbying feature on the same news day).
