## 3. Geopolitics & Policy

**Trade, tariffs, and sanctions mechanics.** Senate Finance Committee ranking member Ron Wyden says Democrats will make rolling back Trump's Canada tariffs their first target once they control Congress in January, with further actions targeting the novel legal authorities (Sections 338, 122, 232 and 301) Trump has used to justify tariffs without prior precedent. The mechanism matters: any legislation would need 60 Senate votes to survive a filibuster and likely two-thirds majorities (290 House, 67 Senate) to override a presidential veto — thresholds Democrats do not currently have. Market implication: tariff policy continuity risk is now a multi-year legislative fight rather than a settled fact, with border-state industries (Michigan, Maine, Alaska) the most exposed. Geopolitical signal: this is the clearest evidence yet that Trump's "broad authority to levy tariffs if he feels national security is threatened" is being directly contested by a newly empowered legislature, not just litigated in courts.

**Institutional and structural dynamics.** Democrats' Senate majority effectively ends Trump's ability to reshape the judiciary for the remainder of his term, with no realistic path to a fourth Supreme Court appointment and the 6-3 conservative majority now hostage to the actuarial reality of two aging justices. Separately, the U.S.-Israel alliance is undergoing what the paper calls its "toughest test in decades": Trump's own vice president cited voter exasperation with the war and the Israel relationship as a factor in the GOP's defeat, and Trump himself briefly threatened (and was reported to have caught Israeli officials off guard) to halt military aid "by the end of this year." Structurally, future U.S. administrations are now more likely to be less willing to shield Israel diplomatically at the United Nations — a capital-flows-relevant shift given Israel's reliance on U.S. diplomatic cover for its credit and investment profile.
