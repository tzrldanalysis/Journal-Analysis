## 3. Geopolitics & Policy

**Trade, tariffs and sanctions mechanics.** USTR Jamieson Greer is expected to unveil, as soon as this week, a new tariff action against China, the EU and 14 other nations following a months-long investigation into industrial-subsidy-driven overcapacity. Mechanism: tariffs will largely track 2025 levels, stack on top of (not replace) existing national-security tariffs on steel, aluminum and autos, and layer onto a separate forced-labor tariff action already in place (10%–12.5% on 60 economies, under court challenge). Nations with existing trade deals — Japan, the EU, South Korea — are expected to see rates near 15% or lower; Vietnam and India face higher rates tied to their deals with Washington; China is set to receive a higher tariff than most others. Market implication: this reopens tariff-escalation risk for exactly the trading partners (EU, Japan, Korea) whose equities and currencies had stabilized after 2025's settlements, and it collides with the EU's own new cap on Chinese hybrid-vehicle imports (350,000 units/year, effective December), raising the odds of a multi-front trade fight rather than a single bilateral one. Geopolitical signal: the reporting states plainly that unveiling this now "could help divert attention from the continuing controversy" around Jensen Huang and Nvidia — trade policy is being used as a political release valve, not purely as an economic one.

Separately, Commerce added Alibaba's Apex Enterprise Solutions and Singapore-based Megaspeed (plus affiliates in Thailand and Malaysia) to the U.S. Entity List over suspected advanced-chip smuggling to China — the same Megaspeed entangled in the Alice Huang/Jensen Huang affair. The mechanism bars U.S. firms from dealing with these companies without a special license. The same article notes Commerce had previously blocked tougher action against Megaspeed for two years "during trade talks," which Bessent publicly criticized — meaning export-control enforcement inside this administration has itself been a site of interagency conflict between Commerce and Treasury, not a settled policy.

**Institutional and structural dynamics.** China's Politburo purge of Shanghai party secretary Chen Jining — the fourth purge at this level in under two years — tightens Xi's control heading into the 2027 party congress and removes a once-plausible successor; a China scholar quoted calls it "a chilling signal... no one other than Xi is indispensable." Russian military signaling against Japan, South Korea and near Taiwan has risen sharply (152 Japanese scrambles this year versus 59 last year) alongside deepening Russia-China coordination, while Robert Kaplan's op-ed argues Trump's rhetoric on Taiwan ("very good negotiating chip") already resembles the Yalta-era abandonment dynamic that could trigger Japanese and South Korean nuclear hedging if the U.S. security guarantee is perceived to soften. The Solomon Islands' new treaty with Australia, explicitly barring foreign military bases, is the latest in a string of U.S.-aligned Pacific-island pacts (following Papua New Guinea, Vanuatu, Fiji) built specifically to contain the kind of Chinese basing access that a 2022 Solomons-China pact had threatened. Huawei's conviction on bank fraud, wire fraud and sanctions violations (acquitted on the racketeering-conspiracy count) lands days after Trump's Shenzhen summit with Xi and is likely to further harden, not ease, U.S.-China tech distrust ahead of sentencing.

## 5. Steel-Man

**Consensus view:** Jensen Huang's reinstatement was vindicated by the market — Monday's record rally proved the board's reversal was correct, and the AI trade is back on track.

**Steel-man:** The vindication argument rests entirely on one trading session that Nvidia gave back most of on Tuesday, closing down 5.9% even as the broader market mostly held its ground — hardly the behavior of a resolved situation. More tellingly, Nvidia's own bondholders didn't participate in the euphoria: the company's most actively traded bonds declined slightly on the very day the stock posted its largest-ever one-day gain, meaning the market that actually prices Nvidia's default and governance risk moved the opposite direction from the equity market. None of the underlying problems that triggered the crisis — the unresolved question of what Huang told or didn't tell U.S. intelligence about Alice Huang, the two active DOJ probes into Nvidia customers for chip smuggling, the SEC investigation, or the structural absence of a succession plan — were resolved by the board's decision; they were simply deferred to Huang's Dec. 3 Senate Banking Committee testimony and House hearings next week. A rally driven by short covering and retail buy-the-dip flow (individual investors bought $3.6 billion of Nvidia this month, making it their top ticker) is a liquidity event, not a verdict on the facts.
