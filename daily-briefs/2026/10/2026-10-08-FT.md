## 1. Today's Themes

**AI infrastructure is migrating to wherever energy and land are cheapest, not wherever demand actually sits.** China's data-centre build-out in Ulanqab, Inner Mongolia — 24GW of operational capacity, more than the rest of Asia combined, with 89 centres built or planned — is explicitly a strategy to outrun US and European power constraints. The same logic runs through Europe's telcos pivoting into domestic AI data centres for "tech sovereignty" (Lex, p18), Australia's grid-scale battery boom being pitched as a "postcard from the future" for hyperscaler demand (Moral Money, p10), and SpaceX seeking $40bn to buy Nvidia chips outright. Four separate sections of today's paper describe the same race by different means. [WEAK SIGNAL] Buried in Lex: Meta's "Jolly" AI savings assistant is starting to move idle retail deposits into money-market funds, a quiet early threat to bank net interest margins that has nothing to do with chips but everything to do with AI's second-order effects.

**Sovereign bond markets are repricing fiscal credibility in the US, UK and France at the same time.** The 30-year US Treasury yield hit 6.04 per cent Thursday, its highest since 2002 (p6), French 10-year yields climbed back to 4.67 per cent from last week's lows as the central bank governor publicly rebuffed a "Trump-style" political attack on his independence (p2), and UK gilts are at their highest in decades. One trader's line captures it directly: "It is really a question of having credible budgetary policies and that's a bit scarce right now globally."

**Brussels is hardening against Beijing and Washington in the same week, on three separate fronts.** China rejected the EU's request to voluntarily curb hybrid-car exports, pushing Brussels toward a "safeguard" tariff that could apply retroactively (p2); Germany blocked a Chinese shipping group's acquisition of a logistics stake in Hamburg, its largest seaport, under Chancellor Friedrich Merz (p1, p2); and the European Commission is separately drafting a broad corporate tax aimed at capturing more Big Tech revenue, a plan the Trump administration has reportedly called dead and threatened to meet with tariffs (p1). None of the three stories cross-reference each other in the paper, but together they describe a Union positioning itself against both poles at once.

**Market breadth is narrowing even as headline indices sit near records.** S&P 500 earnings are forecast to grow 27 per cent this quarter, but the gains are concentrated in a shrinking number of AI-linked infrastructure names (p10) — the same page that reports Japan's Topix is about to eject roughly 700 stocks to reduce free-float distortion. Global indices still fell broadly Thursday (S&P 500, Nasdaq, Dow, Euro Stoxx, FTSE, CAC, Dax all lower) despite the AI-driven earnings optimism.

**Western security and alliance credibility is under visible strain.** Germany's foreign intelligence service (BND) is dealing with the arrest of a former chief over an alleged betrayal and recruitment scandal described in the piece as a "catastrophe for German intelligence" (p2); Russia carried out large-scale strikes on Ukrainian power infrastructure ahead of winter (p3); Houthi forces struck Riyadh and Aden directly (p4); and US Secretary of State Rubio used a European platform to call on allies to uphold "traditional values" against "atrophy, servitude and decline" (p3) — diplomatic framing that reads as pointed toward Europe's own political establishment rather than a shared adversary.

## 2. Finance & Markets

Rates dominate. The 30-year US Treasury yield's move to 6.04 per cent — a 24-year high — is the day's clearest credit signal, and it did not happen in isolation: French 10-year yields are back to 4.67 per cent, UK gilts are at their highest level in decades, and Italian yields extended "a series of wild swings," rising more than 10 basis points. The through-line is explicit in the reporting itself — this is a fiscal-credibility repricing, not an isolated technical move, and it is happening in the US, UK and France simultaneously rather than in one jurisdiction that the others can shrug off.

That bond stress sits uncomfortably next to the AI capex story. Thursday's S&P 500 session fell broadly (semiconductors led losses) even as Goldman Sachs and FactSet both raised earnings forecasts on AI infrastructure spending — a split between what analysts expect from AI-linked earnings and what the market is actually paying for duration risk right now. Lex's oil column (p11) adds a cross-asset wrinkle worth connecting here: futures-quoted crude increasingly diverges from the physical price actually paid, with a Hormuz-crossing risk premium baked in that futures markets no longer capture well — a reminder that headline commodity prices are becoming less reliable proxies for real supply-demand conditions just as rates volatility rises elsewhere.

## 3. Geopolitics & Policy

**Trade and tariffs.** Beijing's rejection of the EU's request to voluntarily curb hybrid-vehicle exports (p2) pushes Brussels toward a "safeguard" tariff mechanism that officials say could be applied retroactively — a harder instrument than the negotiated caps first floated. The market implication is direct cost uncertainty for EU automakers already exposed to China; the geopolitical signal is that Brussels is willing to escalate even against explicit German reluctance, since Berlin fears retaliation against its own auto, chemical and agricultural exports to China. [FT ANGLE] This sits inside a genuinely Brussels-centric framing that a US outlet would likely compress into a single "EU weighs China tariffs" line — the FT treats the German-Brussels disagreement over tactics as the real story, not the tariff itself.

The EU's parallel corporate tax plan aimed at capturing more Big Tech revenue (p1) is the same dynamic from a different angle: a proposal built around a broad revenue threshold rather than naming US tech groups directly, designed to avoid the appearance of singling out American companies while still functionally taxing them, against a Trump administration that has reportedly dismissed the plan and floated retaliatory tariffs. Market implication: continued regulatory overhang on US tech earnings in the EU; geopolitical signal: Brussels is willing to accept transatlantic friction rather than drop the measure.

**Institutional and structural dynamics.** Germany's block of a Chinese shipping group's stake in a Hamburg logistics operator (p1, p2) — needing Chancellor Merz's direct involvement — is the clearest sign yet that Berlin's China posture has hardened on security grounds even as it remains dovish on trade retaliation, a genuine internal contradiction in German policy that the hybrid-tariff story exposes from the other side. Separately, the arrest of a former BND chief over an alleged betrayal and foreign-recruitment scandal (p2) — described in-paper as a "catastrophe for German intelligence" — is a credibility hit to German intelligence-sharing with allies at a moment Berlin is trying to project a firmer security posture. [FT ANGLE] Rubio's speech urging western nations to uphold "traditional values" against "atrophy, servitude and decline" (p3), delivered from Sarajevo and Athens, reads in the FT's telling as implicitly critical of European domestic politics rather than aimed at a shared external adversary — a framing a US outlet covering the same remarks domestically would likely soften or omit. Russia's large-scale strikes on Kyiv's power infrastructure ahead of winter (p3) and the Houthi strikes on Riyadh and Aden (p4) round out a day where multiple fronts of Western security architecture — European intelligence, Ukrainian energy resilience, Gulf shipping security — show visible strain simultaneously.

## 5. Steel-Man

**Consensus view:** The AI capital-expenditure supercycle justifies record equity valuations and the 27 per cent earnings growth forecast for AI-infrastructure-linked S&P 500 names this quarter.

**Steel-man:** Today's edition contains the counter-evidence in three separate places. First, the earnings growth itself is concentrated in "an increasingly narrow range of AI-linked stocks" (p10) — the opposite of a broad-based economic signal. Second, a meaningful share of the capex driving that growth is now financed through credit markets rather than internal cash flow: SpaceX's reported $40bn Nvidia purchase runs through junk-bond and private-credit channels, and Blue Owl's public pivot into insurance "following tumult in private capital" (p11) only makes sense if a credit manager expects stress in exactly the markets funding this build-out. Third, the AI capex cycle is accelerating at the precise moment sovereign borrowing costs are hitting multi-decade highs across the US, UK and France (p6) — rising capital costs should compress the valuation multiples the AI story depends on, not coexist comfortably with them. The steel-man is not that AI demand is fake; it is that the financing structure underneath it looks more fragile, and more exposed to a bond-market shock, than the earnings-growth headline suggests.
