## 6. Prior Under Pressure

**Prior:** U.S. Treasurys are the risk-free global reserve asset, and foreign capital will reliably flood into them during any crisis, preserving America's "exorbitant privilege" of cheap, abundant financing almost regardless of its own fiscal trajectory.

**Pressure:** Today's paper stacks three separate facts against this prior without any one of them individually breaking it: the 10-year yield's climb to a 24-year high and its steepest quarterly rise since 1994; the explicit WSJ column observation that Trump has been "openly talking about the benefit of 'very rapidly' eroding [Treasurys'] real value through inflation" — a direct verbal threat to the real-return promise underlying the reserve-asset thesis; and the simple math that U.S. debt has topped $40 trillion with a deficit over 6% of GDP (versus Bessent's own 3% target), with growth running at 2% against a promised 3%. None of this forces capital flight today — the dollar is at an 18-month high precisely because there's no credible alternative. But "no credible alternative" is a different, weaker claim than "risk-free," and the gap between those two claims is exactly where the prior is now resting.

## 12. One To Watch

The quiet unwinding of the Fairford bomber deployment deserves far more weight than its placement (A8, with a follow-on opinion piece) suggests. A handful of men with vans and petrol cans — not a sophisticated "Spiderweb"-style operation — triggered the hurried withdrawal of roughly a dozen irreplaceable B-1 bombers (the U.S. has only ~45) several thousand miles from an active theater, with some aircraft departing before refueling was even arranged. That is a wildly asymmetric outcome: a crude, possibly unsuccessful plot achieved a strategic withdrawal that a far more capable adversary might not have achieved through direct attack. If this pattern generalizes — and the Pentagon's own global basing review suggests officials think it will — the entire logic of forward-deployed, high-value U.S. air power in Europe and the Middle East is now hostage to a threat (cheap drones, minimal-training saboteurs) that is asymmetric in cost, legally awkward to counter (as the U.K.'s drone-jamming law shows), and essentially unattributable in real time. This is a bigger story than Ukraine's "Spiderweb" raid on Russia, because it demonstrates the vulnerability runs both ways and that NATO's own basing posture, not just Russia's, is now exposed to it.
