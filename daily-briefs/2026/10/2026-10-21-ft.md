## 5. Steel-Man

**Consensus view:** CDS activity on AI-linked debt is primarily sophisticated hedging and relative-value trading by multi-asset managers — a sign markets are maturely managing AI-infrastructure risk, not an early warning of default.

**Steel-man:** The scale and direction of the data argue otherwise. AI-linked debt issuance has grown 4.6x year-over-year to $466bn, a pace of leverage accumulation that historically precedes credit events rather than coincides with stable risk management. Oracle's CDS has more than doubled since January even though Oracle itself hasn't missed a payment or issued a profit warning — spread widening on a solvent, investment-grade-adjacent name is a sign the market is repricing tail risk across the whole AI-financing complex, not reacting to idiosyncratic news. CoreWeave's CDS, at 1,090bp, is pricing economics close to genuine distress, and its equity has already more than halved from May — that is a realized market move, not a hypothetical hedge payoff. Jim Caron's own characterization of the buyer base — part genuine hedgers, part speculators seeking "a cheap hedge to their tech exposure" — means the instrument is now partly self-referential: wider spreads attract more protection-buying, which itself signals more stress, in a reflexive loop that doesn't require any actual default to keep widening. Add Elice's 32% IPO-debut collapse and Firmus's abandoned A$5bn listing, and three independent, realized price events (not just derivative pricing) corroborate the credit market's signal on the same day.

## 6. Prior Under Pressure

**Prior:** French sovereign risk has been largely absorbed by markets — the OAT-Bund spread reflects manageable political noise around a government that, however fragile, retains the capacity to govern and will eventually pass a budget.

**Pressure:** The spread is still widening even as the proximate cause (street violence) recedes — it is now "close to its highest level since 2011," a level that predates the riots and reflects something more structural. Sylvie Kauffmann's column states the deeper problem explicitly: "no serious political decision has been taken for the past two years" since Macron's snap-election gamble failed to produce a majority, and "parliament still has to pass a budget that cuts spending when protesters are demanding the opposite." A government that cannot make fiscal decisions while facing a 119% debt-to-GDP ratio and a 5.4% deficit is a different, harder-to-price risk than street disorder — and the bond market, not the insurance industry or the police, is the instrument currently flagging it most clearly.
