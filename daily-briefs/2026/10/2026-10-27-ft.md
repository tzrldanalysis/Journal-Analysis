Congressional Republicans are now querying the Delaware AIC plan over "anti-competitive regulatory capture," and California wealth-tax campaigners are citing the Meta share restructuring as evidence billionaires will accept real costs rather than relocate.

**Populist strongmen are losing power across Central and South-Eastern Europe in a pattern with no clear successor governance model.** Serbia's Student List protest movement — described today as having "no clear leader, party structure or fully formed programme" — ousted Russia-friendly strongman Aleksandar Vučić after 12 years, explicitly paralleled in Ben Hall's column with Viktor Orbán's defeat in Hungary in April. ## 3. Geopolitics & Policy

**Trade, tariffs and sanctions.** Today's edition is notably quiet on new tariff or sanctions mechanics — the closest analogue is fiscal rather than trade: offshore tax havens (Guernsey, Jersey, Bermuda) are reporting windfall corporate-tax receipts as multinationals choose to stay rather than flee the OECD's Pillar Two 15 per cent minimum-rate regime, with Bermuda forecasting $291mn in its first year of Pillar Two-linked receipts and the OECD projecting a $91bn–$155bn global revenue gain from the regime. The market implication is a quiet validation of the minimum-tax architecture holding up better than the "exodus" scenario multinationals warned of in 2021; the geopolitical signal is that small sovereign jurisdictions are adapting faster than larger economies to a rules-based system originally designed to constrain them.

**Saudi Arabia is raising utility tariffs directly against the backdrop of Houthi attacks and war-driven fiscal strain.** Electricity tariffs rise 5–17 per cent from January 2027, water bills two-to-threefold, with the finance ministry explicitly citing an expected 3.6 per cent GDP decline this quarter "in light of geopolitical developments in the region." Market implication: a GCC sovereign that typically uses subsidy reform as a slow-burn Vision 2030 initiative is now compressing the timeline under war pressure — a credit-relevant signal for Saudi fiscal consolidation pace. Geopolitical signal: the subsidy rollback lands the same week Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport reopened after Houthi missile and drone strikes killed 18 and injured more than 300, underscoring how directly the conflict is now bleeding into fiscal policy.

**Germany is buying Ukrainian-developed cruise missile technology for its own armed forces** — the low-cost "Bars" missile (range to 1,000km, payload to 105kg) — part of a wider pattern of Nato states (Denmark, Estonia, Latvia already signalling interest) sourcing weapons technology from Ukraine rather than the traditional reverse flow. This sits inside Germany's ‡780bn five-year rearmament drive. Market implication: a structural shift in the European defence-industrial base toward Ukrainian production capacity, worth tracking against incumbent European primes. Geopolitical signal: this is NATO's most mass-produced low-cost weapon, built in secrecy under a scheme ("Build with Ukraine") Zelenskyy launched last year — a sign Kyiv's defence-tech sector is becoming an exporter to its own security guarantors even mid-war.

**Second priority — institutional and structural dynamics.** A senior diplomatic flurry — officials from Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey meeting their Iranian counterparts in Tehran — is working to prevent a new round of hostilities in the US-Israeli war with Iran. [FT ANGLE] But Gideon Rachman's column argues this diplomatic track sits in tension with the domestic political incentives driving both Washington and Jerusalem: Trump faces midterms on November 3 having failed to protect Israel from an Iran war he inherited rising prices and American lives lost from, while Netanyahu — whose military campaign has, in Rachman's words, "failed" to achieve regime change — faces a January election where "a renewed war with Iran would give him an electoral boost." The FT's own front-page framing (Rachman's column promoted alongside the Tehran diplomacy story) draws this connective tissue between election calendars and military escalation risk explicitly, where a US-centric outlet would more likely treat the diplomacy and the domestic politics as separate news and opinion items rather than a single causal argument.

Separately, **Serbia's Student List victory over Vučić** raises institutional questions beyond the ballot box: an abandoned Belgrade hotel redevelopment backed by Donald Trump's son-in-law Jared Kushner on the site of a Nato-bombed, Yugoslav-era General Staff building is emerging as an early test of whether the new movement will "willingly relinquish grip on institutions, the security apparatus or their media," per Belgrade political scientist Branislav Radeljić. Vučić himself could face criminal proceedings if evidence of broken law emerges. The structural read: the collapse of a 12-year "competitive authoritarian" system has no clear transition mechanism yet, a live institutional-risk case for European affairs desks.

## 5. Steel-Man

**Consensus view:** The AI infrastructure buildout is justified by its own momentum and backed by the market's deepest pockets — today alone saw Meta raise $95bn, Anthropic disclose $800bn of data-centre commitments en route to a Nasdaq listing, and SpaceX secure $40bn in AI-chip financing from Apollo, all underwritten or cornerstoned by Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom.

**Steel-man:** Lex's own read of the Anthropic prospectus undercuts the consensus from inside the same edition: valuing Anthropic is "a fool's errand" because the business could be worth $584bn in revenue by 2031 if current growth holds, or far less if growth merely halves, and the $81bn H1 net loss — even stripped of the non-cash convertible-note revaluation — still reflects a company burning cash into a governance structure that leaves "ordinary shareholders almost no control" over decisions. The Nasdaq 100 is already down more than 5 per cent over the past month on a semiconductor-led sell-off, meaning public markets are showing signs of AI-capex fatigue at the exact moment issuance is accelerating. Layer on today's credit-market signals — junk yields up 160bp in a year, $2tn of speculative-grade debt maturing by 2028, DWS gating a flagship property fund — and the picture is one where capital is becoming choosier just as the AI buildout's financing needs are shifting from (already-stretched) corporate cash flow to external debt and equity markets. If the market's current enthusiasm reflects momentum rather than analysis, as Lex explicitly argues investors "will work out" valuations without good information, the risk is a repricing event triggered not by a change in AI's promise but by a change in the cost or availability of the capital funding it.

## 6. Prior Under Pressure

**Prior:** AI hyperscaler and AI-lab capital expenditure is effectively self-funding — insulated from broader credit-market stress because the companies involved (Meta, Google, Amazon, Microsoft, Nvidia) generate enormous free cash flow and can absorb capex from existing balance sheets.

**Pressure:** Today's edition shows the funding model shifting decisively toward external capital markets at the same moment those markets are tightening. Meta chose to raise $95bn in new equity rather than fund its "hundreds of billions" in AI infrastructure spend purely from operating cash flow; Anthropic is going public specifically to access public capital for its $800bn in data-centre commitments; SpaceX turned to a private-capital group (Apollo) for $40bn in AI-chip financing rather than internal funds. This is happening as junk bond yields have risen from 6.5 to 8.1 per cent over the past year and $2tn of speculative-grade debt comes due by 2028 — a credit backdrop that makes external AI financing structurally more expensive than it was even six months ago. The prior that AI capex is insulated from credit cycles looks harder to hold when three of the sector's largest players chose external capital markets, not cash flow, as their financing mechanism on the very same day.

## 12. One To Watch

The collision between the AI buildout's shift toward capital-markets financing and a credit cycle that is turning more selective at precisely the wrong moment. Three mega-financings disclosed on a single day — Meta's $95bn stock sale, Anthropic's $800bn-commitment Nasdaq listing, and SpaceX's $40bn Apollo-backed chip financing — are being priced into a market where junk bond yields have risen 160 basis points in a year and $2tn of speculative-grade debt matures by 2028. Lex's own skepticism about Anthropic's valuation and governance, published in the same edition as the financing story itself, is the clearest signal yet that this is not a uniformly bullish consensus even inside the outlet covering it most closely. A stumble in any one of these three financings — Anthropic's listing most of all, given the explicit governance and valuation doubts already on the record — would be the cleanest test to date of whether AI-related credit and equity risk is actually being priced, or simply assumed away by the volume of capital chasing it.

## 13. Research Call Questions

Anthropic's Long-Term Benefit Trust retains 50.1 per cent voting power for founders and family even after listing, with five of seven board seats effectively outside ordinary shareholder control, while Amazon holds roughly a 20 per cent as-converted economic stake capped at 12 per cent voting power.
**Question:** How should we price governance-control discounts into AI-lab IPO valuations when voting power is this structurally decoupled from capital at risk, and does Amazon's capped-voting position change the calculus for how strategic investors value control versus economics?

Brazil's real fell almost 12 per cent and the Bovespa suffered its worst single session since 1999 on a result that pre-election polling had largely priced as the status-quo outcome relative to Bolsonaro's more market-friendly platform.
**Question:** What does the scale of Monday's Brazil repricing tell us about how badly binary election risk is being modelled elsewhere right now — specifically, are desks under-pricing a similar fiscal-credibility shock around Israel's upcoming election or the UK's Autumn Budget?

DWS has capped withdrawal honouring at 10 per cent of requests on its flagship German property fund, joining a sector that has lost ‡18bn in outflows since 2024, echoing prior open-ended-fund gating crises (2016 Brexit vote, 2020 Covid).
**Question:** Is the German open-ended property fund gating a contained, sector-specific liquidity event, or an early warning that broader European commercial real estate marks haven't yet been written down to clearing levels?

Nearly every major buy-side voice quoted today — from BNY to PIMCO to JPMorgan Asset Management — is asking UK Chancellor Healey for a "boring," surprise-free Budget rather than growth measures, an unusually unanimous signal ahead of a major fiscal event.
**Question:** If the Budget delivers exactly the "no surprises" outcome investors are asking for, does that actually arrest the gilt sell-off, or has UK term-premium repricing already decoupled from near-term fiscal news given the structural debt-servicing and inflation backdrop (CPI at 3.5 per cent)?

Junk bond yields have risen from 6.5 to 8.1 per cent over the past 12 months even as nearly $2tn of speculative-grade debt comes due over the next five years, with issuers increasingly "pulling forward" refinancing to beat further rate rises.
**Question:** Which sectors have the least room to refinance into this higher-cost environment before 2028, and does AI-adjacent corporate and structured debt — data-centre financing, chip-supply-chain issuers — sit disproportionately inside that maturity wall?
