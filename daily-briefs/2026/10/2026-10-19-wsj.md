## 3. Geopolitics & Policy

**Trade/sanctions mechanics.** Friday night, Trump signed an order halting implementation of most sanctions under the bipartisan Lindsey Graham Act (Senate-passed 86-11 last month), which would have hit third-country buyers of Russian oil; he also waived sanctions on Russian political/military officials, financial institutions, the energy industry and Moscow's sanctions-evasion "shadow fleet." This followed an Oct. 9 deal to import Russian diesel — a roughly $4.5 billion windfall for Russian energy firms — explicitly framed as fuel-price relief two weeks before midterm elections. Market implication: the move is unlikely to deliver the intended price relief — energy experts quoted in the paper say the volumes are too small to matter and that the Iran war's effect on the Strait of Hormuz, not Russian supply, is the dominant driver of the diesel/jet-fuel spike (crude +$9.56 this week alone, jet fuel +91% since February). Geopolitical signal: Putin has proposed a Trump-Xi-Putin summit, potentially on the sidelines of the Nov. 18-19 APEC summit in Shenzhen — a format that explicitly excludes both Zelensky and Europe, elevates China as power-broker, and feeds Kremlin ambitions for a "second Yalta" sphere-of-influence settlement. Taiwan-watchers see an uncomfortable precedent in great powers negotiating a smaller nation's fate without it present.

**Institutional/structural dynamics.** Zelensky broke from 18 months of courting Trump to publicly criticize him to the FT and British media the same week Ukraine launched its largest drone strike on St. Petersburg since the war began, hitting an oil terminal in Putin's hometown. Separately, restrictions on Ukraine's military-grade SpaceX Starshield service — reportedly originating "from inside the White House" per Reuters, with Trump denying involvement and denying it was Musk — remain partly in place, degrading Ukrainian air-defense coordination with no one taking ownership of the decision (see One to Watch). On the Republican side, Senate candidates in competitive midterm races (Rogers-MI, Sullivan-AK, Hinson-IA) are publicly opposing both the sanctions hold and the diesel deal, signaling real intra-party fracture in races that could decide chamber control.

**Saudi Arabia.** Houthi missile/drone attacks (Iran-supplied, including medium-range missiles reportedly built with Chinese parts) have closed Riyadh's international airport for a week and are now "almost daily." The kingdom's GDP is forecast to contract 3.6% in 2026, oil activity -21.8%, tourism down 5-6%. Washington is providing intelligence only; Israel's offer of direct military assistance was declined given the absence of formal diplomatic relations. ## 7. What Would Have To Be True

**Implication:** Trump's Russia-diesel deal and sanctions waiver meaningfully lower fuel costs before Nov. 3, blunting the "affordability election" narrative hurting Republicans.
**Assumption:** Russian diesel volumes and the broader signal of eased sanctions must move global diesel/fuel prices enough, within roughly two weeks, to register with voters already primed to blame the administration for high prices.
**Fragility:** The paper's own reporting undercuts this — energy experts call the volumes "too small to make much difference," crude actually rose $9.56 this week to $101.41, and jet fuel is up 91% since February on Iran-war dynamics the Russia deal does nothing to address.

**Implication:** The AI-capex-driven profit windfall at suppliers (chips, cooling, power, cabling) converts into durable, non-capex-dependent demand before financing costs force a construction slowdown.
**Assumption:** Enterprise end-customers' willingness to pay for AI compute must reach a level, within the next few years, that sustains supplier margins once the current building wave matures and the temporary capex-linked profit surge fades.
**Fragility:** Every comparable historical capex supercycle cited in the paper — 1790s canal mania, the 1840s British and 1880s American railway bubbles, electrification, radio, fiber/dot-com — delivered real benefits eventually, but with profits arriving later, smaller, and more often captured by users than by the builders of the infrastructure. "This time it's different" is, by the columnist's own framing, the exact phrase that should trigger skepticism.
