**Headline trade-deal numbers are unraveling on contact with the counterparty.**

## 3. Geopolitics & Policy

**Trade, tariffs, and sanctions mechanics.**
The U.S.-South Korea Alaska LNG pipeline framework — $350bn in pledged investment plus $100bn in energy purchases, in exchange for cutting tariffs on most goods to 15% from 25% — collapsed into open disagreement within a day of being announced. Trump touted a "historic $54 billion Korean investment"; the joint fact sheet itself named no dollar figure; South Korea's industry minister Kim Jung-kwan publicly objected that U.S. language "goes far beyond what was agreed." Market implication: credibility risk for every similarly-structured headline trade pact (EU, Japan) that markets have priced as resolved. Geopolitical signal: a close ally pushing back publicly, mid-implementation, is unusual and suggests Seoul is managing its own domestic political exposure (a tight November Senate-adjacent election cycle) more than it is honoring Washington's framing.

Separately, the U.S. is weighing a diesel export ban to ease domestic prices. Mechanism: cutting off a crucial marginal supply source would spike prices for Latin America (90% of its diesel imports are American) and Asia while barely denting U.S. pump prices, because diesel production is capacity-linked to other refined products. Geopolitical signal: this would strain relationships with energy-dependent allies precisely while the administration is trying to hold a sanctions coalition together against Iran.

**Institutional and structural dynamics.**
Iran's oil-export blockade is visibly working as a pressure tool — Hormuz tanker traffic sits at 14.19 million b/d against a 17.13 million prewar baseline, the rial has collapsed roughly 140% annualized, and Trump rejected Iran's latest proposal (reopening Hormuz and resuming nuclear talks in exchange for lifting the port blockade). The Pentagon is sending a third carrier strike group and 9,000-10,000 additional troops, and Trump has privately told aides he expects to resume bombing Iran after the midterms. Structural risk: Iran has a documented history of using "unwitting criminal proxies" for deniable escalation — visible this week in both the IRGC-linked UK terror-plot arrest (target: RAF Fairford, a U.S. bomber staging base) and the ongoing FlyDubai cockpit-stabbing terrorism investigation. Both are unconnected on their face but rhyme with the same proxy playbook during the same war.

European populist realignment is accelerating for reasons that are domestic, not diplomatic: Germany's foreign minister met his Russian counterpart for the first time since 2022, but a German Marshall Fund analyst called it messaging aimed at German voters (42% think Berlin sends "too many weapons" to Ukraine; AfD just posted record East German state-election wins) rather than a genuine Moscow opening — and Lavrov publicly scorned the outreach immediately afterward. France's Le Pen and Mélenchon are both polling strongly ahead of next spring's presidential race on explicitly anti-aid platforms. The structural effect: Europe is shifting its Ukraine rhetoric toward "we seek peace" framing to manage voter fatigue, not because the war's trajectory has changed.

Ethiopia severed diplomatic relations with Eritrea after drone strikes on Addis Ababa, risking the Tigray conflict metastasizing into a direct interstate war — a serious, under-covered African flashpoint with refugee and regional-stability spillover potential.

Hormuz tanker traffic is most of the way back to prewar levels, yet diesel is at record prices in both the U.S. ($6.39/gallon, up ~70% since the war began) and the EU ($9.63/gallon) because refining capacity, not crude flow, is now the binding constraint — and that cannot be expanded by decree or blockade enforcement.
