## 5. Steel-Man

**Consensus view:** The AI capex supercycle is being financed responsibly across global bond markets — "anywhere and everywhere," in PGIM's phrase — without yet threatening a systemic credit event, and equity markets' continued resilience through a multi-decade-high bond yield environment confirms the underlying growth story is sound.

**Steel-man:** The FT's own Big Read supplies the counter-case in its own reporting, not from outside critics. The credit market has already bifurcated into "the AI-related issuers and everyone else," with hyperscaler bonds trading at a discount to A-rated peers "where it wasn't before" — meaning repricing is already under way, just not yet visible in headline spreads. Multiple credit strategists quoted in the same piece call the current dynamic "inevitably a one-way bet" (Morgan Stanley's Scott Schulte) and warn that "the credit story in hyperscalers rests on a single consensus assumption... the minute there's a problem anywhere... the ecosystem is so intertwined" (Peters, PGIM). Apollo's chief economist Torsten Slok puts a specific number on the assumption everyone is making: hyperscaler operating cash flow needs to roughly triple from $600bn to $2tn to justify the debt loads already issued, and if it doesn't, "the risk is that the AI trade weakens, with credit spreads widening, capex plans getting cut and ultimately US GDP growth slowing" — his words, not a bear-case outsider's. Separately, Sheila Bair's own op-ed argues the Fed is deliberately tightening precisely because financial-asset-driven demand (of which the AI capex boom is the largest current example) needs cooling, not accommodating — an explicit statement that policymakers see today's resilience as something to lean against, not validate.

## 6. Prior Under Pressure

**Prior:** Private-credit stress is genuinely abating — falling redemption requests at Ares, Apollo, BlackRock and Blackstone signal that fears of a rising-corporate-default wave were overblown, and the credit cycle has turned a corner.

**Pressure:** The same edition's own numbers undercut the "turned a corner" reading: all four major flagship funds remain capped at 5% of value in quarterly redemptions and are meeting on average less than half of investor withdrawal requests — meaning the "decline in requests" could just as easily reflect investors giving up on an exit that structurally isn't available, rather than genuine conviction the credit is sound. Barclays analyst Peter Troisi's own quote hedges directly: "it's too early to call a bottom on the redemptions but it's clear that the trend is that the headline redemption numbers are declining... it would be a more positive signal if the rate of decline was faster." Meanwhile the much larger, more interconnected AI-linked public/wholesale credit market described in the Big Read is moving the opposite direction — toward higher structural risk, not lower — which the private-credit story doesn't address at all.
