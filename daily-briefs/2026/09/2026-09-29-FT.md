## 1. Today's Themes

**The global rate-cutting cycle has been overwritten by an Iran-war inflation shock, and central banks are hiking into it.** The 10-year Treasury yield hit 5.22–5.27%, its highest since 2007/2008, as the Fed raised rates this month for the first time since 2023 and futures markets now price two more quarter-point hikes by January — "a marked shift from the cuts that had been expected before the US-Iran war." UK 10-year gilts rose to 5.44% (highest since 2007), and French and Italian 10-year yields hit fresh multi-year highs. The trigger was oil: Brent jumped to $108.83 after Trump rejected Iran's proposal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz over the weekend. Cross-reference: this is the backdrop against which Swati Dhingra's op-ed argues the BoE has "little choice but to lean more heavily against domestically generated inflation" from supply shocks it cannot control — a genuinely global phenomenon, not a US-specific one.

**Russia is deliberately probing the seam below Nato's Article 5, and Europe still has no agreed collective answer.** EU defence ministers met yesterday to discuss a proposed "Emergency Security Protocol" that would trigger overlapping decision-making when hybrid attacks (sabotage, arson, drone incursions) hit member states — modelled loosely on Article 4 consultations. Diplomats admit to "widespread confusion" over what the mechanism would actually do, and officials fear it could compete with, and weaken, Nato's own processes. The FT's own editorial (FT View) explicitly calls this urgent: "failure to make a firm response risks only emboldening it to go further." Cross-reference: Putin's parallel decree forcing Russian businesses to self-fund anti-drone defences (with nationalisation threat for non-compliance) is the mirror-image domestic pressure of the same hybrid-war dynamic — the state offloading physical-security costs onto private balance sheets on both sides of the front.

No mention of Taiwan de-escalation in the joint statements, though Xi separately reiterated a threat of conflict over Taiwan "independence." China's rare-earth dominance — the one lever that matters to advanced manufacturing and AI — was not on the table. Cross-reference: Nicolai Tangen's op-ed on eroding shareholder rights (36 per cent of new US listings in H1 2026 carried dual-class structures, up from 44 per cent... in 2020 for voting power retained) describes the same underlying condition from the public-market side — allocators of capital extracting more discretion from the people supplying it, whether through buybacks that concentrate control or lock-ups that don't unwind.

**[WEAK SIGNAL] State security capacity is quietly being repriced onto corporate and household balance sheets, in adversarial and allied economies alike.** Russia's anti-drone decree threatens "temporary management" (i.e., seizure) of any of 700,000-plus "critical infrastructure" companies that fail to self-fund protection — business inquiries to drone-defence consultants reportedly rose fivefold overnight. Neither story references the other, but both describe the same structural handoff: the state's monopoly on protecting people and assets is eroding, and private capital is stepping in to price what's left.

## 3. Geopolitics & Policy

**Trade/tariffs/sanctions mechanics.** The US-China "30-for-30" framework allows each side to import $30bn of goods from the other at reduced tariff rates — China's list of 1,619 favoured US items includes agricultural products (chilled foie gras, rabbit hair, live camels, whales and dolphins for breeding) and coal-based products; the US list of 77 Chinese items ranges from child car seats to fishhooks. This is a narrow, symbolic de-escalation, not a structural reset — rare earths, the actual point of leverage, remain untouched, and China is reported to be preparing to tighten the rare-earth squeeze further even as the diplomatic temperature warms. Separately, Beijing has hit Japan with fresh export controls after PM Sanae Takaichi's comments on a China-Taiwan war scenario — a reminder that the "great powers coexisting" framing from the Xi visit doesn't extend to Japan.

**Second priority — institutional/structural dynamics.** The EU's proposed Emergency Security Protocol for responding to Russian hybrid attacks is the most consequential structural story in today's edition: it would create "overlapping decision-making pathways" triggered by acts of sabotage, arson or drone incursion that fall below Article 5's armed-attack threshold. The debate exposes a real fracture — some members want painful, coordinated responses ("if we are facing painful hybrid threats... our answer should be painful in a similar way," per EU defence commissioner Kubilius); others worry the new mechanism will duplicate, and thereby weaken, Nato's existing Article 4 consultation process. [FT ANGLE]: this section reads as far more urgent and mechanism-focused than a US paper would likely treat it — the FT's own editorial explicitly argues Europe is under-responding.

Poland's central bank standoff is a second structural story: President Nawrocki and PM Tusk are blocking each other's nominees to the National Bank of Poland's management board, leaving it potentially understaffed below its legal minimum, with explicit analyst comparisons to "obvious parallels" with Trump's conflict with the Federal Reserve. This is a genuine emerging-market-adjacent central-bank-independence story inside the EU, not just a US phenomenon.

## 5. Steel-Man

**Consensus view:** Europe's centrist "firewall" will hold against the far right, because proportional representation, the tendency of far-right parties to moderate or fail once in government, and EU-membership constraints all structurally limit populist power (Gideon Rachman, Global Affairs).

**Steel-man:** The mechanism Rachman cites — PR softening far-right power by forcing coalition compromise — doesn't apply to France's presidential system, which today's edition notes is "winner-takes-all": if Le Pen wins next May's runoff, there is no coalition partner to dilute her mandate the way Dutch or Austrian coalition partners diluted the PVV or FPÖ. Germany's own "firewall" depends on the CDU remaining an electorally credible host party, and today's paper documents Merz suffering three consecutive disastrous regional election losses, AfD topping polls in two states, and open CDU speculation about a "chancellor swap" toward Hendrik Wüst — the firewall is a function of CDU strength, and that strength is visibly eroding in real time, not holding steady. Poland, meanwhile, shows that a functioning centrist government doesn't even need a far-right challenger to produce institutional paralysis: Tusk and a populist-aligned president have deadlocked the central bank's board through ordinary cohabitation dysfunction. If the load-bearing institutions can seize up without the far right even winning, "the centre will hold" is a weaker claim than the analytical framing suggests.
