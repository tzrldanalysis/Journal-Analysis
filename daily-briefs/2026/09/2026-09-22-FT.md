## 1. Today's Themes

**A roughly $2tn yen carry trade is showing early signs of a disorderly unwind, and it's the most systemically important story in the paper.** The Bank of Japan raised rates to 1.25% on Friday and signaled more increases; the Fed hiked to 3.75%-4.00% the same week, its first increase in three years. [FT ANGLE] This is a structurally FT-native story — global capital-flow plumbing — that gets no equivalent US-press treatment; a US-centric reader would miss that the same cheap-yen mechanism has been quietly helping anchor US Treasury yields and inflating AI-related equity valuations.

[FT ANGLE] Where WSJ covered Meta's and AMD's AI-driven stock moves as a bullish equity story today, FT's framing is explicitly about whether the credit and financing structure underneath the AI buildout can hold.

**Gulf states are quietly hedging toward Iran even as Washington stays on a war footing.** The UAE's Anwar Gargash called for a "new mindset" in regional diplomacy, Qatar's prime minister described the war as a "wake-up call" requiring the Gulf to "act together" with Iran, and Saudi Arabia has discussed a non-aggression pact with Tehran — all while Riyadh separately battles a fresh Houthi campaign and the US has resisted Saudi requests to join strikes against the rebels. ## 6. Prior Under Pressure

**Prior:** The yen would remain a reliable, effectively permanent source of ultra-cheap global funding, safely anchoring carry trades, US Treasury demand and even AI-equity valuations, given Japan's central bank rate hadn't exceeded 1% since 1995.

**Pressure:** [FT ANGLE] The BOJ raised rates to 1.25% on Friday and signaled further increases; cross-border yen borrowing rose 67% to ¥560tn ($2tn) between December 2021 and March 2026; and Citi data show Japan's total outstanding stock of foreign direct investment has grown from 20% of GDP in 2014 to more than half today, with non-financial corporations now holding more overseas assets than the country's banks, pension funds and insurers combined — a structurally yen-short position built by real economic actors, not just speculators. Barry Eichengreen of UC Berkeley states plainly that the yen's "funding currency days are over now that Japanese interest rates are finally beginning to normalise." The fragility was visible even in Monday's ostensibly calm session: the yen tumbled after the BOJ's own Friday hike, prompting talk of a "rate hike race" between Tokyo and Washington rather than the orderly normalization the prior assumed.
