## 1. Today's Themes

**The bond market is repricing for a hawkish Fed hiking into a supply shock, not a demand boom.** The 10-year Treasury yield jumped above 5.1% Wednesday — its biggest one-day rise in over a year and the highest level since 2007 — after a hot flash PMI (58.4, fastest services/manufacturing growth in years), hawkish comments from Fed Governor Barr ("further policy adjustments are likely to be needed"), a weak 5-year auction that forced primary dealers to absorb an unusually large share, and an Iran-driven oil spike (Brent back above $103). The Dow fell 352 points (-0.68%), the S&P 500 lost 0.75%, and the Nasdaq dropped 1.13%. Cross-reference: this is the same day the AI capex story (below) is making the front page — the two are connected, since data-center investment is a major source of the "resilient growth" the PMI is capturing.

**The AI infrastructure build-out is now the largest single-industry capital commitment in US history, and it is increasingly debt-financed.** A front-page analysis (citing Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh/Brookings) puts total 2025-2029 data-center and related spending at $10.3 trillion — 36% of the entire US corporate/household capex base and larger, as a share of the economy, than any prior infrastructure boom since 19th-century railways. FactSet data shows Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle alone spending $4.2 trillion 2025-2028, a "growing share" financed by debt via off-balance-sheet vehicles with "little public reporting." [WEAK SIGNAL] Buried in the same package: import prices for computer/semiconductor components are still rising and the value of household equity/mutual-fund holdings is 50%+ above trend — two data points suggesting the AI-fueled wealth effect is now doing real macro work, which is itself a source of the "reflation" the bond market is punishing.

**Trump is running two overlapping foreign-economic-pressure campaigns — against China's AI ambitions rhetorically, and against Iran's oil exports literally — that are colliding with each other's market effects.** Xi Jinping arrived in Washington for a state visit as Trump used his UN speech to reject a "globalist scheme" to regulate AI (rebranding the term itself to "superintelligence," now mandated State Department usage), even as Anthropic's Amodei and OpenAI's Altman publicly call for UN Security Council-level cooperation on AI safety. Simultaneously, the Iran war's energy shock is forcing a domestic diesel-export-ban fight that pits the oil lobby (API, AFPM, Chevron's Wirth, Phillips 66's Lashier) against the president's midterm-driven impulse to cap prices — with Energy Secretary Wright reportedly steering toward a "voluntary" compromise.

**Private credit is quietly extending its reach from corporate lending into mainstream consumer credit-card underwriting.** JPMorgan — the largest US card issuer — is exploring routing credit-card applicants it rejects to private-credit firms (Blue Owl, Blackstone, KKR, Sixth Street) who would take on the underwriting risk behind the scenes. This is a structural extension of a decade-long private-credit expansion into bank balance-sheet risk that started in corporate lending and is now reaching subprime consumer paper.

## 2. Finance & Markets

**Rates.** The 10-year Treasury yield's jump above 5.1% is the story of the day, driven by a stack of surprises rather than one clean catalyst: the S&P Global flash Composite PMI beat (58.4 vs. 56.0 in August, the strongest reading since early 2020 excluding the reopening spike), Barr's hawkish speech, and a poorly-received 5-year note auction that forced primary dealers — who are required to bid — to take down an unusually large share, signaling weak organic demand. Futures markets now show a "tossup" on an October hike but strong conviction on at least one more hike by year-end. Treasury's own $6bn buyback operation, sized up to try to cap yields, "had little effect on the selloff" — a signal that Bessent's tool kit is being tested and found wanting in real time.

**Credit.** Investment-grade spreads were mixed: Jefferies Financial, Daimler Truck, Protective Life and General Electric paper tightened, while Caterpillar Financial and Iberdola International widened. High-yield price decliners included Paramount Global, Occidental Petroleum, Navient and ADT Security — Paramount's slide is notable given the WBD-Paramount deal's own regulatory clearance news the same day (see Business section), suggesting the market is pricing integration/leverage risk into the combined entity even as the deal itself clears.

**FX/commodities.** The dollar was little changed (EUR $1.1386, yen 158.32) while gold fell sharply, down $57.60 to $4,261.30 — an unusual pairing with a bond selloff, suggesting real-yield-driven repricing rather than a generalized inflation-hedge bid. Brent crossed $103 intraday on Iranian President Pezeshkian's comments that Tehran won't fully reopen the Strait of Hormuz while sanctions remain, directly linking the geopolitical and rates stories: oil-driven inflation fear is now a primary bond-market input.

**Equities/cross-asset.** All three major indexes fell, but the "risk markets seem to be handling this fairly well — it really seems to be a rate market problem," per Loop Capital's Scott Kimball — an important distinction: credit and equity vol have not (yet) cracked the way a genuine growth scare would produce. The AI capex story is the credit-risk one to watch: with data-center spending increasingly financed off-balance-sheet through private-credit and bank lending, "if AI doesn't generate enough revenue to service the debt raised to build data centers, the fallout could ripple through the financial system" (Van Nieuwerburgh).

## 3. Geopolitics & Policy

**Trade/tariffs/sanctions mechanics.** The live fight is over whether Trump follows through on backing a US diesel-export ban. Mechanism as reported: no formal rule yet, but Trump verbally endorsed the idea Tuesday; the oil-and-gas lobby (API, AFPM, a 30+ group letter including the Business Roundtable) mobilized within a day, and Energy Secretary Wright is steering toward a "voluntary" limit that would fall short of an outright ban. Market implication: an actual ban would spike domestic diesel supply (US refiners currently export a large surplus) while foreign buyers scramble, which is exactly why the market is already pricing risk into refiners and product freight. Geopolitical signal: this is now overtly a midterm-driven policy lever, with Treasury Secretary Bessent (seen as opposed to a full ban) and Interior's Burgum publicly at odds with unnamed hawks inside the administration who want action before November.

**Institutional/structural dynamics.** Trump's UN posture explicitly rejects multilateral AI governance ("globalist scheme") even as US-based AI labs (Anthropic, OpenAI) publicly ask the UN Security Council to coordinate on safety — a split between the administration and its own domestic AI champions that's rare and worth tracking. Separately, the State Department's mandated switch from "artificial intelligence" to "superintelligence" in all diplomatic communications is a small but telling bureaucratic signal of how far rebranding-as-policy has gone. On sovereignty: Canada's Carney told the Journal he's deepening EU cooperation (critical minerals, satellites, Erasmus+) specifically to hedge against US tariff pressure, framing "integration" itself as being "weaponized" — a direct rebuttal of Trump's "51st state" rhetoric that nonetheless stops short of full EU membership.

**Iran war.** Pezeshkian's UN speech was defiant but drew a thin diplomatic crowd; the same day, a cargo ship was attacked in the Strait of Hormuz, killing an Indian sailor — the most serious such attack in recent days. Indirect US-Iran talks on the UN sidelines produced "no major breakthrough" per Secretary Rubio. The linkage to markets is direct and immediate via the oil price and the diesel-export debate above.

## 5. Steel-Man

**Consensus view:** The hot PMI, hawkish Fed commentary and weak Treasury auction confirm the economy is running too hot for the Fed's comfort, justifying further hikes and the accompanying bond selloff.

**Steel-man:** The PMI strength is concentrated and possibly narrow — it's arriving alongside, and plausibly driven by, an unprecedented, debt-financed AI capex supercycle rather than broad-based demand, meaning the "overheating" the Fed is reacting to may be a sectoral phenomenon dressed as a macro one. New Fed Chairman Warsh notably didn't submit dot-plot projections at all last week, and only 16 of 18 officials see even one more hike — hardly a unanimous hawkish consensus, and Barr is one voice, not the committee. Equity and credit markets, per Kimball's comment, are shrugging off the yield spike rather than pricing recession or systemic stress, which is inconsistent with a genuine overheating scare — it looks far more like a technical rates story (weak auction, oil shock) than validation that the economy needs materially tighter policy. And the oil-price component of the "inflation" the Fed is fighting is a supply shock from the Iran war, which higher rates cannot address and which a diesel export ban would only worsen.
