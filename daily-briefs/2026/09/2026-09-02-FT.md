## 1. Today's Themes

## 5. Steel-Man

**Consensus view:** Today's synchronized global bond sell-off reflects government fiscal irresponsibility — the FT View states directly that "governments are spending beyond their means" and that the remedy is fiscal discipline, not financial repression.

**Steel-man:** Matt King's Markets Insight column, printed in the same edition, offers a materially different diagnosis using the same market moves as evidence. King argues the dominant driver isn't sovereign profligacy but private-sector AI financing that has shifted from cash-funded to credit-funded: "borrowing on this scale, which is indifferent to the cost it pays, drives up yields for everyone else — homeowners and even governments." He notes tech issuance is doing the opposite of what rising yields should produce (issuance normally falls as yields rise; here it hasn't), a price-insensitivity that dwarfs government-deficit dynamics in marginal impact even though it's smaller in absolute scale. If King is right, the FT View's prescription — welfare and pension reform, resisting unfunded tax cuts — treats a symptom rather than the underlying mechanism, and would do comparatively little to arrest the sell-off. Crucially, King's own framing complicates the bullish "AI growth justifies higher yields" narrative too: it "does not account for the strain already visible in the pockets of credit that are funding the AI boom" and "overlooks how the spending has been paid for."

## 6. Prior Under Pressure

**Prior:** Rising bond yields reflect market confidence in AI-driven growth — a benign real-yield story in which "higher yields... are just a reflection of higher expectations for long-run growth on the back of the AI investment boom," per the bull case King summarizes before dismantling it.

**Pressure:** King identifies three specific holes in this reading from within today's own market data: it fails to explain the strain already visible in AI-boom credit markets; it fails to explain the simultaneous revival of "debasement trades" betting on a weaker dollar (which should not accompany a genuine growth-optimism story); and it "grants permanence to an arrangement that is anything but" by ignoring that the financing mix has shifted from self-funded cash to price-insensitive credit over just three years. Separately, the FT View's own alarm about financial repression — flagging Bessent's Treasury buybacks as an attempt to suppress rates artificially — is itself evidence that policymakers do not trust markets to clear this "benign" story without intervention, which is inconsistent with a true confidence narrative.
