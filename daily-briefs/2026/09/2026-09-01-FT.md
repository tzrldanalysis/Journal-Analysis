## 1. Today's Themes

**Iran's economic war has re-escalated into a shooting war, and the FT's Gulf-centric framing shows the sanctions regime cracking at the edges, not tightening.** Trump vowed to "hit them hard" after Iran fired missiles at US assets in Jordan in retaliation for a US strike on Larak Island in the Strait of Hormuz — the US said it believed the IRGC was preparing to seed the waterway with mines, "ending a month of relative calm in the war." Separately, the FT's Dubai dispatch shows the campaign to isolate Tehran economically is leaking: UAE-Iran trade of nearly $50bn a year has been curtailed but not stopped, with an Iranian businessman describing routing shipments to Bandar Abbas via "fake destinations on official documents" — "we are bypassing the blockade in the same way we have been bypassing sanctions... not much has changed." [FT ANGLE] This ground-level Gulf trade-evasion reporting has no WSJ equivalent and undercuts the market's read of sanctions as tightening pressure.

Bailey is explicitly pushing back on the US's hands-off posture (Trump's executive order stopped short of giving government power to block model releases) ahead of the Xi-Trump September 24 meeting where AI regulation cooperation is expected to be discussed.

**Swiss regulatory capture is playing out in real time, and UBS is winning.** Influential Swiss upper-house lawmakers backed a compromise that would let UBS meet roughly half of its foreign-subsidiary capital requirement with additional tier-1 bonds rather than pure CET1 equity — cutting the incremental common-equity ask from UBS's own claimed $20bn to an estimated $400mn (plus ~$16bn in new AT1 issuance, per JPMorgan). This is a live test of whether the "too big to fail" lessons from Credit Suisse's 2025 collapse survive contact with a bank whose balance sheet the FT describes as exceeding the size of the Swiss economy.

**Israel's West Bank policy has crossed from "creeping" to what the FT's own editorial calls de facto annexation — and it is being priced by no market.** Defence Minister Israel Katz ordered the army to prepare to transfer law-enforcement powers in the West Bank to police, extending civilian administration over occupied territory; more than 1,400 settler attacks have occurred this year against a 2025 total of 1,836 (the highest since UN records began); a September tender for 1,200 housing units in the E1 corridor threatens to bisect the territory and foreclose a contiguous Palestinian state ahead of Israeli elections in October. **Institutional and structural dynamics.** Bailey's G20 letter is a structural signal about regulatory capacity, not a single-event warning: "many jurisdictions do not have the protocols in place to manage the development, release and deployment of advanced frontier AI models." This sits alongside the Xi-Trump September 24 meeting as a structural marker: whichever framework the US and China informally agree on for AI oversight will likely become the de facto global standard, given the G20's own admission of a protocol vacuum elsewhere.

Romania's political paralysis is a second structural story: a €770mn funding loss tied to a hard EU deadline is a live case study in how post-pandemic recovery-fund conditionality is now colliding with domestic coalition politics across the periphery, with Warsaw-based analyst Kamil Calus noting the crisis is unfolding "amid a pile-up of overlapping crises" including Russian drone incursions along Romania's Ukraine border and Iran-war-linked energy price pressure.

## 5. Steel-Man

**Consensus view:** The multi-year Iran sanctions and blockade regime, reinforced by UAE and Gulf-state alignment with Washington, is steadily strangling Tehran's economy and will eventually force capitulation or regime change from within.

**Steel-man:** The FT's own Dubai ground reporting undercuts this. Trade between the UAE and Iran ran close to $50bn annually before the war and, while curtailed, has not stopped — Iranian businessmen are routing goods through Oman and using falsified shipping documents to reach Bandar Abbas, with one stating flatly "not much has changed." Dubai's own regulatory response has been soft-touch (bureaucratic fines, not full closures), and analysts quoted in the piece are explicitly "sceptical whether the economic pressure campaign can succeed." If the mechanism of pressure — trade denial — is this porous at the point of enforcement, the market's assumption that sanctions intensity translates linearly into regime stress deserves more skepticism than it is currently getting. The domestic-strain evidence (Ghalibaf-style warnings, cited elsewhere in today's coverage) may reflect genuine internal fracture, but it is happening despite leaky sanctions enforcement, not because of airtight enforcement — a materially different causal story with different implications for how much further escalation (mining Hormuz, direct US strikes) actually buys.
