## 5. Steel-Man

**Consensus view:** The synchronized global bond selloff — Japan's 10-year at a three-decade high, the UK's 30-year at a 28-year high, the US 30-year at 5.28% — signals an emerging sovereign debt crisis and fiscal reckoning across the developed world.

**Steel-man:** Today's own Review & Outlook column makes the opposing case explicitly: this is "hurray for the bond market," a healthy normalization after two decades of financial repression that let governments borrow cheaply and distort capital allocation — citing Illinois' 2010 pension bonds priced at 3.854% and California's still-unfinished bullet train financed at 2.193%, both evidence that "investors weren't properly pricing credit risk," not that debt was actually sustainable at those levels. A 10-year Treasury in the 4–5% range "was normal before the financial panic and the pandemic." Crucially, equities have "mostly shrugged off" the bond selloff and currency markets remain "relatively orderly" despite four major sovereign markets repricing simultaneously — itself evidence that this is disciplined repricing rather than disorderly panic. Rising yields, on this reading, are the market doing exactly what it's supposed to do: restore price signals and start disciplining "spendthrift governments" before the reckoning becomes acute rather than after.

## 6. Prior Under Pressure

**Prior:** The Fed can continue treating Iran-war-driven energy inflation as a transitory supply shock and hold rates steady until the conflict resolves.

**Pressure:** Governor Barr's own framing sets a much lower bar than "prove it's not transitory" — he says the Fed "should act decisively to raise rates" unless new data show price pressures easing, full stop. Chairman Warsh's Jackson Hole framing was explicit that the traditional case for looking through energy shocks requires the absence of "second- or third-order effects," and he says those effects are now visible: 54% of the Fed's preferred inflation-basket goods are up more than 3% year-over-year, and core inflation ran hot for five straight months before a brief improvement in June and July that hasn't convinced him "the underlying trend was improving." The war has already lasted six months against an original assumption of "weeks." Markets have stopped waiting for the Fed to confirm this shift — investors are pricing a hike "if not in September, then by December, raising the stakes no matter what the central bank does this month," per today's own reporting. The market has effectively already priced out the transitory framework regardless of what the Fed says next.

## 7. What Would Have To Be True

**Implication:** The Fed raises rates at the September 15–16 meeting (or by December at the latest) despite the underlying inflation shock being geopolitically, not domestically, driven.

**Assumption:** Fed officials must conclude that oil-driven inflation has crossed from a first-round supply shock into generating durable second- and third-order effects — wage demands, broader price pass-through — rather than remaining a one-time, mean-reverting cost shock.

**Fragility:** A meaningful de-escalation in the Strait of Hormuz — even a partial one — would let oil retreat from its 13%-in-a-month run-up and remove the primary input driving both the inflation case and the "second/third order effects" argument, reopening the door to the Fed reverting to its wait-and-see posture and undercutting the entire rate-hike repricing the bond and FX markets have already built in.

**Implication:** The global sovereign-bond selloff stays "orderly" — allowing central banks to keep tightening into an oil shock without triggering a disorderly deleveraging event in equities.

**Assumption:** Demand at sovereign debt auctions continues to absorb both the AI-driven surge in corporate bond issuance and rising government deficit financing needs without central banks having to step in as buyers of last resort, and equity investors continue to treat the yield move as a valuation input rather than a systemic threat.

**Fragility:** MUFG's Derek Halpenny states the risk directly — sustained higher rates could "prick" what he calls a bubble in AI-driven stocks, and once that repricing starts, the same investors currently absorbing bond supply calmly could reverse into a self-reinforcing selloff across both asset classes simultaneously, which is the disorderly-unwind scenario every source quoted today is implicitly betting against.
