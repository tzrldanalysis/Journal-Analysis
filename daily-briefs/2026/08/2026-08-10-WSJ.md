## 3. Geopolitics & Policy

The Strait of Hormuz standoff is the day's clearest trade-and-sanctions mechanism story. Iran's Supreme National Security Council, dominated by hardliners reporting to Khamenei, is reviewing an Oman-brokered draft to open new shipping lanes and gradually reopen the strait — but only in exchange for a permanent end to the war, a lifted naval blockade, full troop withdrawal, removal of all sanctions, unfreezing of Iran's foreign assets, and war reparations. Washington has said it won't accept Iranian tolls or restrictions on shipping traffic and won't send taxpayer money to Tehran, though a deal is unlikely without unfreezing some assets, possibly with conditions attached. The market implication is already visible in Section 2: crude fell 7.67% last week despite Saturday's missile strike on a U.A.E.-flagged ship, the 15th such attack since the war began. The geopolitical signal is sharper: Tehran's negotiators may believe Washington wants out badly enough to accept a face-saving exit without a nuclear resolution, which is exactly the dynamic U.S. officials privately describe — Trump has told aides Iran likely can't revive its nuclear program after the strikes on its three primary sites, and is reportedly willing to walk away without a deal at all.

A second mechanism worth isolating: Spain's decision to impose border checks on arrivals from Italy (effective through September 7) is a direct retaliation for Italy's own restrictions on Spanish arrivals, imposed after 72,000 people breached Ceuta's border with Morocco. This is a bilateral suspension of Schengen free movement between two member states, not a bloc-wide response to an external migration surge — a mechanism the EU hasn't used against itself before. Spanish intelligence's working theory is that the surge was spontaneous, catalyzed by a misread Supreme Court ruling and amplified on WhatsApp and Facebook, rather than orchestrated by any state actor. The structural signal is that the EU's crisis-management machinery is conspicuously absent from the dispute as reported — Brussels isn't named as mediating between two members openly suspending free movement against each other.

On the institutional side, Netanyahu's public rejection of the second phase of Trump's Gaza peace plan — disarmament sequencing, Israeli withdrawal, governance handover to the Board of Peace — is a rare open break with Washington that arrives as Netanyahu faces likely defeat in Israel's next general election and as U.S.-Israel relations have already frayed over diverging goals on Iran. Separately, Ukraine's drone campaign against Wildberries, Russia's dominant e-commerce platform, is functioning as deliberate economic warfare: the company owes Russian banks more than $10 billion, has lost up to $5 billion of inventory since the strikes began, and a Kremlin-discussed bailout risks rippling through Sberbank and VTB. And the extradition of Irish crime boss Daniel Kinahan from Dubai to Ireland — following the U.A.E.'s 2022 placement on the FATF's anti-money-laundering gray list — is a structural marker of the U.A.E.'s slow move away from being a haven for sanctioned and criminal capital, relevant to any desk pricing U.A.E. counterparty and compliance risk.
