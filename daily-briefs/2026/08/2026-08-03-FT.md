## 1. Today's Themes

## 3. Geopolitics & Policy

**Trade/tariffs/sanctions mechanics:** Opec+ (seven countries led by Saudi Arabia and Russia) will raise collective production by 188,000 b/d from September, fully unwinding the 2023 supply cuts — the first full unwind since oversupply-era restraint began. The stated mechanism is de-escalation-contingent: the group explicitly called for reduced attacks on energy infrastructure and framed open sea routes as "essential for world markets," tying the supply decision directly to the Iran conflict's trajectory. Market implication: incremental barrels are being added into a market still pricing conflict risk, meaning the net price effect depends entirely on whether the diplomatic track holds — a bet, not a certainty. Geopolitical signal: Gulf producers are now actively lobbying Washington on strike/no-strike decisions, a level of direct leverage over US military posture in the region that is itself a structural shift.

**Institutional and structural dynamics:** The mechanism behind Trump's stand-down is unusually explicit for a security decision — Qatar, Saudi Arabia and the UAE called US counterparts directly, with Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman personally engaging Trump, to stop a strike over fears that renewed fighting would draw in Iranian-backed Houthis attempting to close the Bab al-Mandab strait (a second chokepoint beyond Hormuz). This is Gulf states exercising direct crisis-management leverage over US strike decisions — a structural change in who sets the pace of Middle East de-escalation. Iran's acting defence minister's dismissal of US rhetoric as "psychological operation and warfare" the same day is the tell that Tehran does not view this as a genuine off-ramp, which strains the "perimeters of a deal" framing considerably.

Separately, the EU's AI Act transparency rules took effect yesterday, with the Commission gaining power to fine companies up to 3% of global turnover or €15mn for non-compliant general-purpose AI systems, and postponing high-risk system rules to December 2027 under industry pressure. [FT ANGLE: the FT gives disproportionate space to the tension between Brussels' stated goal of accelerating AI adoption to compete with the US and China, and the compliance burden the same rules place on EU-based companies like Zalando — a structural read on Europe's competitiveness dilemma that a US outlet would likely treat as a straightforward regulatory-compliance story.]

## 5. Steel-Man

**Consensus view:** US corporate earnings are "rock solid" (JPMorgan Asset Management's own words), with 47.4% year-on-year growth and breadth across 8 of 11 S&P sectors confirming the US economy is absorbing higher energy costs and rates without real damage.

**Steel-man:** The earnings breadth is more fragile than the headline suggests. A meaningful share of the strongest results — Google's quadrupled net income, driven partly by investment gains rather than operating leverage; the energy sector's "windfall" from petrol prices Trump's own Iran war pushed above $4/gallon — are one-off or policy-driven rather than organic. The consumer picture underneath the aggregate is bifurcated by design: Joe Brusuelas of RSM explicitly attributes resilient spending to the "wealth effect" concentrated among asset-owning households, while P&G's CFO describes lower-income consumers living "paycheque to paycheque" and Albertsons just cut guidance on precisely that group's retrenchment. A market that is simultaneously bidding up the S&P on breadth and repricing the long end of the curve on Fed-credibility concerns (bear steepener, 30-year at 2007 highs) is not describing an economy in robust health — it's describing one where headline strength and underlying strain are being priced as separate, unconnected facts, which rarely persists.

## 6. Prior Under Pressure

**Prior:** Central bank communication under a new Fed chair, even if more reserved in style than predecessors, retains market trust in the institution's competence and its ability to guide inflation expectations.

**Pressure:** Warsh's press conference — described as an near-refusal "to explain the Fed's decision to do nothing, despite persistently above-target inflation" — triggered a bear steepener whose only recent precedent, per the FT's own data going back to 2000, is the November 2010 QE2 announcement. That is an extraordinary market reaction to a decision to do nothing. HSBC explicitly frames this as the market "adding a credibility premium" — language usually reserved for sovereign or corporate credit risk, not monetary policy communication. The comparison the FT draws to Volcker and Greenspan's own reticence is meant to soften the read, but the market reaction itself argues the comparison doesn't hold: those chairs were quiet against a backdrop of established credibility; Warsh is quiet in his first press conference, against a backdrop the market hasn't yet decided to trust.
