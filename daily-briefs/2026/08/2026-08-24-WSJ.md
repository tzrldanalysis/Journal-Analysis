## 3. Geopolitics & Policy

**Canada.** Mechanism: Section 338 of the 1930 Tariff Act, invoked for the first time by any president, imposing 50% tariffs on $20 billion of Canadian goods (~5% of Canada's U.S.-bound exports), effective Saturday. Near-deal terms that collapsed: steel/aluminum tariffs from 50% to 25%, auto tariffs from 25% to 15% (matching Japan/South Korea rates) — killed by Commerce Secretary Lutnick's push to hold downstream aluminum products at 50% and a last-minute U.S. demand to strip truck-tariff relief specifically affecting Ford's Ontario plant. Carney retaliates dollar-for-dollar on steel, dairy, and appliances starting Sept. 8. Geopolitical signal: the U.S. also raised Canadian French-language/culture protections and Canada's freedom to strike third-country trade deals — sovereignty-coded demands that go beyond tariff arithmetic and explain why Carney is calling this "a question of sovereignty" rather than a negotiation.

**Russia/Kaliningrad.** Mechanism: officers flown from St. Petersburg to Kaliningrad last month to plan housing, feeding, and arming procedures for a potential regional mobilization; no decision made, likely deferred until after late-September parliamentary elections. Driver: manpower shortfalls (Ukrainian drone advances have cut average Russian front-line soldier lifespan to minutes/days/weeks) against a stalling Donbas objective (Kramatorsk/Sloviansk). Western intelligence flags the Suwalki Gap — the Kaliningrad-to-Belarus land corridor through Poland/Lithuania — as a seizure scenario in any escalation, which is NATO Article 5-adjacent territory. Market implication: Georgian and Armenian property prices are already rising in anticipation of a fresh outflow wave; a full mobilization would, per Eurasia Group's Kolyandr, put Russia's budget under "immense pressure."

**Iran.** President Pezeshkian and parliamentary speaker Ghalibaf are both publicly prioritizing an end to the war and economic stabilization over hardliner defiance, six months into the conflict, as the naval blockade and sanctions regime sink the currency. Both the June and August peace frameworks collapsed specifically over Strait of Hormuz traffic control. Analyst Azizi calls it a "vicious circle" — economic pressure forces concessions, but concessions look like a humiliating climbdown, which Citrinowicz says makes escalation more likely, not less, despite the conciliatory rhetoric.

**Germany.** AfD is polling for an outright majority in the Sept. 6 Saxony-Anhalt state election — which would produce Germany's first far-right state government since World War II and break the 13-year cross-party "firewall" pact nationally. The party is under domestic-intelligence surveillance as an extremist organization and is polling ahead of Chancellor Merz's CDU nationally. The federal government's response — a joint "Bauhaus Manifesto" pledge with Unesco and business groups — reads as defensive messaging, not containment.

**Cuba.** Rubio's sanctions campaign is targeting the military-linked GAESA elite directly (asset sanctions, visa/residency revocations) rather than broad embargo, betting on a negotiated elite exit (Moscow, Ankara, or Managua retirement) over regime collapse. The O'Grady op-ed's "Day Zero" framework — diaspora-capital-funded reconstruction rather than U.S.-taxpayer-funded nation-building — signals the policy conversation has moved from "if" to "how" a transition gets financed.

**Hong Kong.** Chow Hang-tung was convicted of inciting subversion for organizing Tiananmen memorial vigils, in a national-security trial with no jury available to her. It's a further data point on the one-way ratchet of Hong Kong judicial independence — relevant to any rule-of-law risk premium attached to HK-domiciled capital markets.
