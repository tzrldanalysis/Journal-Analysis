## 1. Today's Themes

**AI capex has become the load-bearing pillar of U.S. growth optics, and the paper's own numbers show how narrow that foundation is.** Oxford Economics and JPMorgan estimate AI-related investment and the wealth effect from AI-driven equity gains together account for roughly a quarter to a third of recent GDP growth; household net worth hit $174–175 trillion largely on stock gains. But a chunk of that "investment" is imported semiconductor and memory-chip content from Taiwan (~$20 billion in five months), meaning some of the reported growth is import absorption, not domestic productive capacity — a point Oxford's Michael Pearce makes explicitly.

This connects directly to the oil story: Brent fell toward $84 on hopes of a Hormuz reopening, even as Iran's foreign ministry denied any talks were underway — the defense buildout is being financed on the assumption of continued conflict risk even as markets price a resolution.

**Credit is quietly repricing the AI boom that equities are celebrating.** Hyperscaler investment-grade bond spreads (Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, Oracle, SpaceX) widened almost a quarter point over the past month per Barclays, even as the S&P and Dow hit records. Cross-reference: this sits uneasily against the Buffett Indicator hitting a record ~230%+ of GDP the same day — equity and credit investors in the same AI-linked names are sending diverging signals about how much confidence to extend.

**Institutional and structural dynamics.** The U.S.-Japan currency intervention is a genuine departure: Washington has now used its own balance sheet (via a Treasury-collateral repo facility) to backstop an ally's currency, a tool previously reserved for genuine financial-stability emergencies (1998 Asian crisis, 2011 post-earthquake Japan) or client-state support (Argentina under Milei). Walter Russell Mead's Global View column frames this within a broader pattern: China, Russia, Iran and North Korea are all operating in the "gray zone" below the threshold that triggers strong public reaction — cyberattacks on U.S. water utilities linked to Iranian hackers, Chinese harassment in waters around Japan/Philippines/Taiwan, migration used as a political weapon (evident today in the Ceuta crisis). ## 6. Prior Under Pressure

**Prior:** U.S. currency policy defaults to non-intervention — floating exchange rates are the accepted global regime, and currency-market stress in other countries is those countries' problem to manage domestically.

**Pressure:** The joint U.S.-Japan intervention, plus the new Treasury repo facility letting Japan tap dollars against UST collateral, is a materially different posture — Washington has now put its own balance sheet behind a foreign currency's stability, and Bessent explicitly cast it as extending a pattern that began with Argentina under Milei. The Review & Outlook piece argues this intervention "won't have a lasting effect" without BOJ tightening, yet BOJ faces domestic political pressure (from Takaichi's own coalition) to keep easing — meaning the U.S. may be underwriting yen stability with tools that Tokyo's own politics are simultaneously working against. That combination — an ostensibly market-driven FX regime now backstopped by U.S. Treasury credit, deployed selectively for favored governments — strains the credibility of "floating exchange rates" as an actual description of policy.
