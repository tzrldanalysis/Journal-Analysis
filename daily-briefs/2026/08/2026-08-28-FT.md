## 3. Geopolitics & Policy

**Sanctions mechanics: Gazprombank Luxembourg bond-swap scheme.** Four senior Gazprombank Luxembourg executives allegedly bought discounted, sanctioned Russian Eurobonds personally, then swapped them with Gazprom Moscow for full-value replacement bonds denominated to route around the EU's post-2022 sanctions architecture — routing through National Settlement Depository, exploiting a window before bond-level sanctions took full effect. Market implication: it confirms the EU's securities-settlement sanctions perimeter still has structural seams four years in, which is bullish for continued legal/compliance-driven demand at law firms and compliance vendors, and a tail risk for any bank with residual CIS-adjacent custody relationships. Geopolitical signal: Luxembourg's regulator moving to a criminal referral (rather than a fine) signals the EU is willing to escalate individual accountability, which raises the cost of quiet sanctions arbitrage across the bloc's financial centres.

**Frozen Russian assets: the €200bn mobilisation push.** Sweden, the Netherlands, Poland and Spain are pushing Brussels to find a legally durable structure to lend Ukraine funds backed by immobilised Russian central bank assets held largely at Euroclear in Belgium. Belgium blocked an earlier iteration over exactly the liability-sharing question that remains unresolved: who absorbs legal and retaliation risk if Russia successfully challenges the seizure in international courts or retaliates against EU assets still in Russia. Market implication: an EU-wide backstop, if agreed, is a source of announced-but-undeliverable headline risk until Belgium's legal concerns are structurally resolved, not just politically overridden. Geopolitical signal: continued failure to resolve this by year-end would be read in Kyiv and Moscow alike as evidence the EU's unity on financing Ukraine has a hard ceiling.

**Nato burden-shifting: Colby's "sea of red."** The Pentagon's policy chief told European counterparts their capability gaps are colour-coded "red" across Nato's own assessment framework, tied this to a push for the 3.5% GDP spending target by 2035, and reiterated the administration wants European troop-posture decisions "concluded by December." [FT ANGLE] this is treated as an ongoing alliance-management story with institutional stakes for Nato's assessment credibility itself, where US coverage tends to frame the same events as a binary burden-sharing complaint.

**Second priority — institutional dynamics: Uefa vs Infantino/Fifa, Kushner nexus.** Uefa is preparing a criminal complaint in Switzerland against Fifa president Gianni Infantino over the sale of a 20% stake in Fifa's commercial rights vehicle to Thrive Capital (founded by Joshua Kushner, brother of Trump son-in-law Jared Kushner) at what Uefa's documents call a valuation "absurdly low." This is a governance-and-conflicts story with direct optics relevance given the Kushner family's parallel role in the administration's foreign and Gulf-investment policy.

## 6. Prior Under Pressure

**Prior:** Sustained high US Treasury yields mechanically tighten financial conditions in emerging markets, triggering capital flight, currency crises and forced rate hikes across EM — the standard taper-tantrum-era playbook.

**Pressure:** Lex states plainly that "Treasuries paying rates of 5% are not crushing emerging markets" this cycle. EM local-currency bonds are rallying; Brazil, South Africa and Thailand real yields are among the most attractive in years, and capital is flowing toward EM debt rather than away from it. The mechanism assumed to transmit US rate stress into EM stress — dollar funding scarcity forcing EM central banks to defend currencies with punishing hikes — has not activated, arguably because EM central banks' inflation-targeting credibility is now higher than in prior cycles, breaking the old linkage between US term premium and EM crisis risk.

## 12. One To Watch

The Gazprombank Luxembourg case is worth tracking past today's headline because it is the first EU sanctions-circumvention case escalated to a criminal referral rather than resolved through a fine or supervisory letter, and it names individual senior bankers rather than the institution alone — a meaningfully different enforcement posture than the EU has shown through most of the post-2022 sanctions period. If Luxembourg's prosecutors move to actual charges, it sets a template other national regulators (particularly in smaller financial centres with residual Russia-linked custody business) will have to react to, and raises the compliance cost of any EU bank retaining ambiguous CIS-adjacent relationships regardless of formal sanctions-list status.
