## 5. Steel-Man

**Consensus view:** Today's global bond selloff — 30-year Treasurys above 5.3% for the first time since 2007, French OATs at 4.1%, German bunds at 3.26% — is a dangerous, disorderly repricing driven by fiscal panic and Fed-credibility doubts that threatens to choke off growth.

**Steel-man:** The Journal's own opinion page directly contradicts its own front page here: "Apparently 'rout' is the new word for interest rates returning to a historical norm... yields finally are reverting to normal after the low-rate era." PGIM's Robert Tipp calls it explicitly "a normalization." Truist's Keith Lerner notes markets "have been able to overlook the increase in yields... because we've had this earnings boom" — and today's tape backs that: the S&P sits within 1.4% of its August 13 all-time high, Home Depot beat and raised, and BofA's Fund Manager Survey shows overweight-equity positioning at a five-year high. AEI's Michael Strain reframes the causal story entirely: "the issue is not so much the rising interest rates... the issue is the deficit" — a structural, slow-moving problem, not a market-panic one. If growth and earnings stay resilient enough to absorb higher discount rates (as they are currently doing), this is capital repricing correctly after a decade and a half of artificially suppressed rates, not a crisis.

## 6. Prior Under Pressure

**Prior:** US extended military deterrence reliably backstops allies and international shipping lanes — adversaries that attack US partners or interests face proportionate American military response, which is what preserves the credibility of the security umbrella over the Gulf, Northeast Asia, and beyond.

**Pressure:** Iran has attacked seven UAE-flagged vessels this month with zero US military retaliation, despite Trump's own earlier promise to strike an Iranian bridge or power plant for every ship hit — and Gulf officials are on the record saying the absence of response "is encouraging Iran to increase its attacks." Simultaneously, Trump this week unilaterally shrank a joint US-South Korea military drill ahead of a prospective, unconditioned summit with a North Korean leader whose "nuclear arsenal has only grown stronger" since three prior summits accomplished nothing. Both cases show actual military posture being subordinated to transactional diplomacy in the same week, in two theaters that anchor the credibility of the same security guarantee. The blockade strategy may be working economically against Iran ($18B in added annual shipping costs per Iran's own trade officials) — but Gulf allies are pricing the credibility cost, not the economic one, and that cost is compounding.

## 7. What Would Have To Be True

**Implication:** Washington's blockade-and-sanctions strategy forces Iran to de-escalate in the Strait of Hormuz without further US military engagement.

**Assumption:** Iran's leadership is economically rational and internally cohesive enough that accumulating trade costs ($18B annually, per Iran's own trade-body head) outweigh the domestic political cost of appearing to capitulate, and that Gulf states remain willing to keep absorbing interim shipping losses (7 UAE vessels hit this month) without acting unilaterally.

**Fragility:** A bulk carrier attack this week already killed a chief engineer. One further high-casualty incident could force either a Gulf state's independent military response or reverse US restraint regardless of the blockade's economic effectiveness — flipping the escalation clock from Washington's control to Tehran's or Abu Dhabi's.

**Implication:** The global bond selloff resolves as an orderly "normalization" rather than a disorderly, fiscal-crisis-driven repricing.

**Assumption:** Growth and corporate earnings stay resilient enough to absorb structurally higher discount rates without triggering a forced-seller event, and no large, leveraged borrower — sovereign, AI-capex-financing tech issuer, or private-credit-heavy insurer — is pushed into disorderly deleveraging.

**Fragility:** The same opinion piece invoking "normalization" cites the UK gilt crisis (2022) and SVB's collapse (2023) as precedents for exactly this kind of transition producing forced-seller events. Today's paper discloses ~$1 trillion of insurer capital now sitting in private credit, and one specific instance of opacity inside that system — Walter/Guggenheim's ~$20B of undisclosed affiliate loans — is already under federal investigation, with ratings agencies threatening downgrades. A forced unwind there, at the same moment discount rates are resetting higher system-wide, is the plausible transmission mechanism from "normalization" to "event."
