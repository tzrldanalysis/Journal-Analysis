## 6. Prior Under Pressure

**Prior:** The US dollar's reserve-currency status is structurally unassailable — a fixed feature of the global financial system that other currencies' problems (yen weakness, rupee weakness) only reinforce by comparison.

**Pressure:** Eichengreen's column makes the case directly: the US Treasury choosing to fund a currency intervention with euro sales rather than dollar sales, combined with Japan opting to tap a Fed repo facility rather than sell Treasuries outright, both signal that Washington itself doesn't want foreign central banks liquidating dollar reserves right now — precisely because the market for that liquidation (long-end Treasuries, already at 2007-high yields) can't comfortably absorb it. "Central banks are accustomed to holding foreign reserves in dollars because markets in US Treasury securities are liquid. But not now, at least not in unlimited quantities," Eichengreen writes, concluding "reserve diversification is apt to gather steam." This is a structural read, not a cyclical one — India's simultaneous $40.8bn diaspora-deposit rupee defense (rather than straightforward FX-reserve deployment) is offered as a second, independent data point of countries finding creative ways around direct dollar-reserve drawdowns this same week.

**Implication 2:** The joint US-Japan yen intervention, paired with expected BOJ rate hikes, durably arrests yen weakness and stabilizes the currency without further US-Treasury-market spillover.
**Assumption:** The Bank of Japan actually delivers meaningfully faster rate hikes — which requires the BOJ to prioritize currency stability over PM Takaichi's stimulus-oriented political preferences, and requires the Fed to hike first (or signal it clearly) to create room for the BOJ to follow, per the "so-called signalling effect" logic in the FT's own markets coverage.
**Fragility:** BOJ Governor Ueda has, per the FT's sourcing, not "match[ed] his words with deeds" on rate-hike urgency despite obvious yen-weakness concern, and analysts (State Street's Masahiko Loo) are on record saying "we all know that the interventions are only buying time... the real heavy lifting is going to fall on the Bank of Japan."
